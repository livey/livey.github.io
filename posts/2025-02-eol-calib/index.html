This article develops end-of-line (EOL) camera extrinsic calibration and calibration-board placement for autonomous-vehicle camera rigs, including bird’s-eye-view applications.

There are two linked optimization problems: estimate camera and board poses from measurements, then choose physical board positions and orientations that make the camera estimates more precise. Camera intrinsics are assumed known, and absolute extrinsics require a world-frame anchor.

The presentation follows this order:

  1. General calibration problem formulation: variables, coordinate conventions, measurements, priors, and identifiability.
  2. Solver: sparse factor-graph bundle adjustment for the pose estimates.
  3. Board-position and orientation optimization formulation: camera uncertainty, design variables, constraints, and optimization methods.
  4. Experiments from simple to complex: one camera and one board, two-camera cases, a multi-camera workspace, and a vehicle rig.

Geometric preprocessing, ray-plane derivations, and optional design formulations are collected in appendices. The detailed ADMM formulation and solver benchmarks are in Appendix E: ADMM and Bundle Adjustment Comparison.

1. General Calibration Problem Formulation#

For multiple boards and cameras, the observation structure should be defined at the marker-point level. Since each camera may see only a subset of the markers on a board, the primitive observation is a camera-point edge:

$$ (c,p)\in\mathcal E \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{camera }c\text{ observes marker point }p. $$

A board-camera graph is only a compressed graph obtained after grouping all observed points on the same board. The general model uses known board-frame marker coordinates, measured world-frame marker coordinates when available, and image observations. Reprojection residuals share the corresponding physical board pose; the primitive measurements remain camera-point observations.

First define the estimation problem independently of its solver or the eventual board-placement design.

1.1 Sets and Observation Graph#

Let there be $B$ boards and $C$ cameras.

Boards:

$$ \mathcal B=\{1,\dots,B\}. $$

Cameras:

$$ \mathcal C=\{1,\dots,C\}. $$

Board $b$ has known marker points:

$$ \mathcal I_b=\{1,\dots,N_b\}. $$

Marker $i$ on board $b$ has known board-frame coordinate:

$$ q_{bi}\in\mathbb R^3. $$

For a planar checkerboard or marker board, usually

$$ q_{bi}= \begin{bmatrix} x_{bi}\\ y_{bi}\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Define the global point set:

$$ \mathcal P= \{p=(b,i): b\in\mathcal B,\ i\in\mathcal I_b\}. $$

For point $p=(b,i)$, define

$$ \beta(p)=b $$

as the board containing point $p$, and define

$$ q_p=q_{bi}. $$

The camera-point observation graph is

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal G=(\mathcal C\cup\mathcal P,\mathcal E), } $$

where

$$ \boxed{ (c,p)\in\mathcal E \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{camera }c\text{ observes point }p. } $$

For each observation edge $(c,p)$, the measured image point is

$$ u_{cp}\in\mathbb R^2. $$

Camera $c$ has known intrinsic matrix

$$ K_c. $$

Define the neighborhoods:

$$ \mathcal N(c)=\{p:(c,p)\in\mathcal E\}, $$$$ \mathcal N(p)=\{c:(c,p)\in\mathcal E\}, $$$$ \mathcal P_b=\{p\in\mathcal P:\beta(p)=b\}. $$

1.2 Pose Variables and Coordinate Convention#

Use a uniform local-to-world pose convention for both boards and cameras. $T^B_b$ denotes the pose of board $b$, and $T^C_c$ denotes the pose of camera $c$.

For board $b$:

$$ \boxed{ T^B_b= \begin{bmatrix} R^B_b & t^B_b\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(3) } $$

maps board-local coordinates to world coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ \bar X_p^W= T^B_{\beta(p)}\bar q_p. } $$

Here

$$ \bar q_p= \begin{bmatrix} q_p\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Define the Euclidean extraction matrix

$$ P_3= \begin{bmatrix} I_3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Equivalently,

$$ \boxed{ X_p^W= P_3\bar X_p^W= R^B_{\beta(p)} q_p+t^B_{\beta(p)}. } $$

For camera $c$:

$$ \boxed{ T^C_c = \begin{bmatrix} R^C_c & t^C_c\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(3) } $$

maps camera-local coordinates to world coordinates. Therefore, to project a world point into camera $c$, use the inverse camera pose:

$$ \boxed{ \bar X_{cp}^C = (T^C_c)^{-1}\bar X_p^W. } $$

where $\bar X_p^W=[(X_p^W)^\top,1]^\top$.

Explicitly,

$$ (T^C_c)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} (R^C_c)^{\top} & -(R^C_c)^{\top}t^C_c\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, $$

and

$$ \boxed{ X_{cp}^C = (R^C_c)^{\top} \left( X_p^W-t^C_c \right). } $$

This is consistent with treating both the board and camera variables as local-to-world poses. The usual computer-vision world-to-camera extrinsic still appears, but now it is written explicitly as $(T^C_c)^{-1}$.

1.3 Projection Model#

For point $p=(b,i)$, the homogeneous world coordinate is

$$ \boxed{ \bar X_p^W = T^B_{\beta(p)}\bar q_p. } $$

The camera-coordinate point is

$$ \boxed{ \bar X_{cp}^C = (T^C_c)^{-1}T^B_{\beta(p)}\bar q_p. } $$

and its Euclidean coordinate is

$$ \boxed{ X_{cp}^C = P_3 (T^C_c)^{-1}T^B_{\beta(p)}\bar q_p \in\mathbb R^3. } $$

Define $\Pi$ as the perspective-division operator, which converts a three-component homogeneous image coordinate into a two-dimensional pixel coordinate:

$$ \boxed{ \Pi\left( \begin{bmatrix} y_1\\y_2\\y_3 \end{bmatrix} \right)= \begin{bmatrix} y_1/y_3\\y_2/y_3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y_3\ne 0. } $$

The product $K_cX_{cp}^C$ gives homogeneous image coordinates. Applying $\Pi$ divides the first two components by the third, so the projected pixel is

$$ \boxed{ \hat u_{cp} = \Pi\left( K_cX_{cp}^C \right). } $$

For camera $c$, let $f_{x,c}$ and $f_{y,c}$ denote the horizontal and vertical focal lengths, respectively, measured in pixels. Let $(c_{x,c},c_{y,c})$ denote the principal-point coordinates in pixels, where the optical axis intersects the image plane. The subscript $c$ identifies the camera. For the standard zero-skew pinhole model, the intrinsic matrix is

$$ K_c= \begin{bmatrix} f_{x,c} & 0 & c_{x,c}\\ 0 & f_{y,c} & c_{y,c}\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Writing

$$ X_{cp}^C= \begin{bmatrix} X\\Y\\Z \end{bmatrix}, $$

gives

$$ \boxed{ \hat u_{cp} = \begin{bmatrix} f_{x,c}X/Z+c_{x,c}\\ f_{y,c}Y/Z+c_{y,c} \end{bmatrix}. } $$

With the usual last row $[0,0,1]$ of $K_c$, the denominator in $\Pi(K_cX_{cp}^C)$ is the camera-frame depth $Z$. A visible point must have $Z>0$.

For a camera-frame point $X^C\in\mathbb R^3$ and a known intrinsic matrix $K$, define the camera pixel-projection operator

$$ \pi_K(X^C)=\Pi(KX^C). $$

Thus $\Pi$ performs perspective division, while $\pi_K$ includes the intrinsic matrix as well. For camera $c$, write $\pi_{K_c}$. These pinhole equations assume undistorted image coordinates. For fisheye or strongly distorted automotive cameras, replace $\pi_K$ by the calibrated camera projection model.

For a world-frame point, define the projection shorthand that also includes the camera pose:

$$ \pi_c(X^W;T^C_c) = \pi_{K_c}\left(P_3(T^C_c)^{-1}\bar X^W\right), $$

where $\bar X^W=[(X^W)^\top,1]^\top$. The reprojection residual is

$$ \boxed{ r_{cp}(T^C_c,T^B_{\beta(p)}) = \Pi\left( K_c P_3 (T^C_c)^{-1} T^B_{\beta(p)} \bar q_p \right)-u_{cp}. } $$

If the image measurement covariance is

$$ \Sigma_{u,cp}\in\mathbb R^{2\times2}, $$

the whitened residual is

$$ \boxed{ e_{cp}(T^C_c,T^B_{\beta(p)}) = \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} \left[ \Pi\left( K_c P_3 (T^C_c)^{-1} T^B_{\beta(p)} \bar q_p \right) - u_{cp} \right]. } $$

1.4 Board and World Measurement Priors#

In the original EOL setup, some board marker coordinates may be measured in the world frame. Let

$$ z_p\in\mathbb R^3 $$

be the measured world coordinate of point $p$. Define the set of points with world measurements as

$$ \mathcal Z\subseteq\mathcal P. $$

The board/world residual is

$$ \boxed{ r^z_p(T^B_{\beta(p)}) = R^B_{\beta(p)} q_p+t^B_{\beta(p)}-z_p. } $$

With covariance

$$ \Sigma_{z,p}, $$

the board prior term is

$$ \boxed{ \phi_b(T^B_b) = \frac12 \sum_{p\in\mathcal P_b\cap\mathcal Z} \left\| R^B_b q_p+t^B_b-z_p \right\|_{\Sigma_{z,p}^{-1}}^2. } $$

You may also have camera pose priors, rig priors, or a fixed reference camera. Write those directly on the camera pose. Let $\bar T^C_c$ be the prior or nominal camera pose. The operator

$$ \operatorname{Log}: SE(3)\rightarrow\mathbb R^6 $$

is the Lie-group logarithm that converts a relative pose into a 6D tangent-vector error, usually ordered as three translation components and three rotation components. For example:

$$ \boxed{ \psi_c(T^C_c) = \frac12 \left\| \operatorname{Log} \left( (\bar T^C_c)^{-1}T^C_c \right) \right\|_{\Sigma_c^{-1}}^2. } $$

1.5 Centralized Calibration Objective#

The cleanest problem is:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \min_{\{T^B_b\},\{T^C_c\}} &\quad \sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E} \frac12 \left\| \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} \left[ \Pi\left( K_c P_3 (T^C_c)^{-1} T^B_{\beta(p)} \bar q_p \right) - u_{cp} \right] \right\|^2 \\ &\quad+ \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\phi_b(T^B_b) + \sum_{c\in\mathcal C}\psi_c(T^C_c). \end{aligned} } \tag{P} $$

This is the point-level multi-board, multi-camera bundle-adjustment problem.

The graph edge is $(c,p)$, but the residual depends on

$$ T^C_c \quad\text{and}\quad T^B_{\beta(p)}. $$

So the raw observation graph is camera-point, while the variable graph is camera-board because each point is rigidly attached to a board.

This shared board-generated point is how multi-camera consistency enters the bundle-adjustment problem. If two cameras $c_1$ and $c_2$ observe the same point $p$, both reprojection residuals use the same world point

$$ X_p^W=R^B_{\beta(p)} q_p+t^B_{\beta(p)}. $$

Thus the two camera observations are coupled through the same board pose and the same physical marker point, rather than through two independent 3D point variables or an extra pairwise cross-camera residual.

With the uniform local-to-world convention, the board-to-camera relative transform is

$$ \boxed{ T_{CB}^{(c,b)} = (T^C_c)^{-1}T^B_b. } $$

This maps board $b$’s local coordinates into camera $c$’s local coordinates, so the projection can also be written as

$$ \boxed{ \hat u_{cp} = \Pi \left( K_c P_3 T_{CB}^{(c,\beta(p))} \bar q_p \right). } $$

1.6 Gauge Freedom and Identifiability#

Without an absolute anchor, each connected component has an $SE(3)$ gauge freedom.

For any

$$ H\in SE(3), $$

the transformation

$$ \boxed{ (T^B_b)'=HT^B_b, } $$$$ \boxed{ (T^C_c)'=HT^C_c } $$

leaves every relative camera-board projection unchanged:

$$ ((T^C_c)')^{-1}(T^B_b)' = (HT^C_c)^{-1}(HT^B_b) = (T^C_c)^{-1}H^{-1}HT^B_b = (T^C_c)^{-1}T^B_b. $$

Therefore, every connected component needs at least one of the following:

$$ \text{one fixed board pose,} $$$$ \text{one fixed camera pose,} $$$$ \text{sufficient world-frame board-point measurements,} $$

or

$$ \text{sufficient camera/rig priors.} $$

Connectivity alone is not enough. The observations must also be geometrically nondegenerate: enough non-collinear board points, reasonable viewing angles, positive depth, and no nearly rank-deficient configurations.

2. Solver: Sparse Factor-Graph Bundle Adjustment#

With the measurement model and gauge fixed, the solver estimates the poses for a given set of observations. It does not yet decide where to move the physical boards.

2.1 Solver Choice#

Sparse factor-graph bundle adjustment is the recommended solver for this centralized EOL calibration problem. The detailed ADMM derivation, including point-copy and pose-copy consensus variants, is given in Appendix E.

2.2 Factor-Graph Structure#

The centralized problem is also a factor graph.

Variables:

$$ \{T^B_b\}_{b=1}^B, \qquad \{T^C_c\}_{c=1}^C. $$

Factors:

$$ f_{cp}(T^C_c,T^B_{\beta(p)}) $$

for every observation edge $(c,p)\in\mathcal E$.

Board priors:

$$ \phi_b(T^B_b). $$

Camera priors:

$$ \psi_c(T^C_c). $$

GTSAM describes factor graphs as bipartite graphs connecting factors to the variables they depend on, and emphasizes that such sparse structure is exploited by nonlinear optimization methods.

This is arguably the cleanest implementation model: one factor per camera-point observation, with the point’s board membership determining which board pose variable the factor touches.

2.3 Sparse Updates and Schur Elimination#

Solve the centralized objective using sparse nonlinear least squares over camera and board pose increments.

The problem is exactly:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \min_{\{T^B_b\},\{T^C_c\}} &\quad \sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E} \frac12 \left\| \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} \left[ \Pi\left( K_c P_3 (T^C_c)^{-1} T^B_{\beta(p)} \bar q_p \right) - u_{cp} \right] \right\|^2 \\ &\quad+ \sum_b\phi_b(T^B_b) + \sum_c\psi_c(T^C_c). \end{aligned} } $$

Use translation-first left pose increments, expressed in the world frame:

$$ T^B_b\leftarrow \exp(\delta\xi_b^\wedge)T^B_b, $$$$ T^C_c\leftarrow \exp(\delta\eta_c^\wedge)T^C_c. $$

Section 3.4 derives the measurement Jacobians using this same convention. Linearize every residual:

$$ e_{cp} \approx e_{cp}^0 + J_{B,cp}\delta\xi_{\beta(p)} + J_{C,cp}\delta\eta_c. $$

Deriving the Damped Normal Equations#

Stack all whitened image residuals and board/camera prior residuals at the current estimate into $r\in\mathbb R^m$. Stack the free camera and board pose increments into $\delta\in\mathbb R^n$, omitting fixed poses. Here $m$ is the number of scalar residuals and $n$ is the number of free scalar pose parameters. Let $J\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ be the Jacobian with respect to these increments, evaluated at the current estimate. The linearized residual vector is

$$ r(\delta)\approx r+J\delta, \qquad r=r(0). $$

The nonlinear least-squares objective is therefore approximated locally by

$$ \min_\delta\frac12\|r+J\delta\|^2. $$

Because this first-order residual approximation may be inaccurate for a large pose update, add a quadratic penalty on the update:

$$ \min_\delta q(\delta), \qquad q(\delta)= \frac12\|r+J\delta\|^2+ \frac{\lambda}{2}\delta^\top D_{\mathrm{LM}}\delta. $$

Here $\lambda\ge0$ is the damping parameter, and $D_{\mathrm{LM}}\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ is a positive diagonal damping matrix that sets the penalty scale for each increment coordinate. If translation and rotation increments have been scaled to comparable units, a simple choice is $D_{\mathrm{LM}}=I_n$, the $n\times n$ identity matrix. A common curvature-based choice is

$$ (D_{\mathrm{LM}})_{jj}= \max\left((J^\top J)_{jj},\varepsilon\right), \qquad j=1,\ldots,n, $$

where $\varepsilon>0$ is a small positive floor in the chosen parameter scaling. This keeps the damping penalty positive even for a coordinate whose Jacobian column is zero.

Expanding the quadratic gives

$$ q(\delta)= \frac12r^\top r+ \delta^\top J^\top r+ \frac12\delta^\top \left(J^\top J+\lambda D_{\mathrm{LM}}\right)\delta. $$

Differentiate with respect to $\delta$ and set the gradient to zero:

$$ \nabla_\delta q= J^\top r+ \left(J^\top J+\lambda D_{\mathrm{LM}}\right)\delta=0. $$

Rearranging yields the damped normal equations:

$$ \boxed{ \left(J^\top J+\lambda D_{\mathrm{LM}}\right)\delta = - J^\top r. } $$

Setting $\lambda=0$ gives the Gauss-Newton normal equations. Positive damping improves conditioning and penalizes large updates; the Levenberg-Marquardt method adjusts $\lambda$ according to how well a trial step reduces the nonlinear objective. The damping penalty is used to compute the update and is not a measurement prior. Camera-information and covariance calculations later in the article use the undamped measurement-and-prior normal matrix.

Schur Elimination#

For this linear solve, define the damped system matrix and gradient vector as

$$ H=J^\top J+\lambda D_{\mathrm{LM}}, \qquad g=J^\top r. $$

Each image residual touches only one board pose and one camera pose. Since $D_{\mathrm{LM}}$ is diagonal, damping preserves this sparsity. Partition $\delta$ into the stacked camera increments $\delta C$ and board increments $\delta B$, and partition $H$ and $g$ in the same order:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} H_{CC} & H_{CB}\\ H_{BC} & H_{BB} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \delta C\\ \delta B \end{bmatrix} = - \begin{bmatrix} g_C\\ g_B \end{bmatrix}. $$

If there are no camera-camera priors, $H_{CC}$ is block diagonal over cameras before Schur fill-in. If there are no board-board priors, $H_{BB}$ is block diagonal over boards before Schur fill-in.

You can eliminate boards:

$$ \boxed{ \left( H_{CC} - H_{CB}H_{BB}^{-1}H_{BC} \right) \delta C = - g_C + H_{CB}H_{BB}^{-1}g_B. } $$

Then back-substitute:

$$ \boxed{ \delta B = - H_{BB}^{-1} \left( g_B+H_{BC}\delta C \right). } $$

Or eliminate cameras instead, depending on whether there are fewer cameras or fewer boards.

This is the same structural idea behind Schur methods in bundle adjustment. Ceres’ documentation describes bundle adjustment as minimizing reprojection error over cameras and 3D points, and its solver documentation explains that bundle adjustment has a special block structure that can be exploited with Schur complement methods.

2.4 Initialization and Engineering Pipeline#

Use this hierarchy:

Best if centralized

Use sparse factor-graph bundle adjustment:

$$ \boxed{ \min_{\{T^B_b\},\{T^C_c\}} \sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E} \text{reprojection error} + \text{board priors} + \text{camera/rig priors}. } $$

This gives the best local convergence and avoids ADMM penalty tuning. In a centralized EOL station, it is better than ADMM as the production solver; the detailed rationale and benchmark comparison are in Appendix E.

If distribution is mandatory

Use ADMM only when camera-local ownership, communication limits, or distributed computation prevent a centralized solve. Appendix E contains the complete point-copy and pose-copy formulations.

Best engineering pipeline

  1. Initialize board poses from world measurements, if available.
  2. Initialize cameras using PnP against current board-generated world points.
  3. Run sparse centralized BA if possible.
  4. Use ADMM only if the system must be distributed, parallelized by camera/board, or too large for a single optimizer.
  5. Optionally use robust losses or outlier rejection on image residuals.

The estimation model can be summarized as follows:

$$ \boxed{ (c,p)\in\mathcal E \quad\text{is the primitive observation,} } $$$$ \boxed{ X_p^W=R^B_{\beta(p)} q_p+t^B_{\beta(p)} \quad\text{is the hard board constraint,} } $$$$ \boxed{ u_{cp} \approx \Pi\left(K_cP_3(T^C_c)^{-1}\bar X_p^W\right) \quad\text{is the camera measurement model.} } $$

Use this convention consistently:

$$ \boxed{ T^B_b: \text{board } b \rightarrow \text{world} } $$$$ \boxed{ T^C_c: \text{camera } c \rightarrow \text{world} } $$$$ \boxed{ T_{CB}^{(c,b)} = (T^C_c)^{-1}T^B_b: \text{board } b \rightarrow \text{camera } c. } $$

Everything else, including ADMM, sparse Schur bundle adjustment, and factor-graph optimization, is a solver strategy for this same constrained geometric estimation problem.

3. Board-Position and Orientation Optimization Formulation#

The outer optimization chooses the physical position and orientation of each board. Its score comes from the reprojection measurements and priors in (P), not from numerical damping or solver-specific penalties.

The objective value itself is not a good placement guide. For a hypothetical board placement with perfectly predicted observations, the expected reprojection residual is zero almost everywhere. The useful placement information is in the local curvature of the objective: the reprojection Jacobian and the normal matrix or information matrix.

3.1 Design Variables and Lower-Level Calibration#

Board placement is an outer design problem around the calibration estimator. The important distinction is between the commanded physical placement of a board and the pose estimated inside calibration:

$$ \begin{aligned} S_b &\in SE(3) \quad\text{is the designed physical placement,}\\ T_b^B &\in SE(3) \quad\text{is the board pose estimated by calibration.} \end{aligned} $$

Let

$$ s = \{S_b\}_{b=1}^B $$

be the board-placement design, and let

$$ x = \left( \{T_c^C\}_{c=1}^C, \{T_b^B\}_{b=1}^B \right) $$

be the lower-level calibration variables. For a fixed placement design $s$, the measurement graph, visibility, image uncertainty, and marker projections all depend on where the boards are placed. Write the visible camera-marker edge set as $\mathcal E(s)$.

The lower-level calibration problem is the same joint bundle-adjustment problem used throughout this article:

$$ x^\star(s) \in \underset{x}{\arg\min}\; \mathcal L(x;s), $$

with

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathcal L(x;s) &= \sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E(s)} \frac12 \left\| \Sigma_{u,cp}(s)^{-1/2} \left[ \Pi\left( K_c P_3 (T_c^C)^{-1} T_{\beta(p)}^B \bar q_p \right) - u_{cp}(s) \right] \right\|^2 \\ &\quad+ \sum_{b=1}^B \phi_b(T_b^B;S_b) + \sum_{c=1}^C \psi_c(T_c^C). \end{aligned} \tag{LL} $$

Here $\beta(p)$ maps marker point $p$ to its board, and $\bar q_p$ is the homogeneous board-frame marker coordinate. The term $\phi_b(T_b^B;S_b)$ connects the estimated board pose to the commanded physical placement. A pose-prior model is

$$ \phi_b(T_b^B;S_b) = \frac12 \left\| \operatorname{Log} \left( S_b^{-1}T_b^B \right) \right\|^2_{\Sigma_{S,b}^{-1}}. $$

If board marker corners are surveyed in the world frame, the same idea can be written as a world-point prior:

$$ \phi_b(T_b^B;S_b) = \frac12 \sum_{p\in\mathcal P_b\cap\mathcal Z} \left\| R_b^B q_p + t_b^B - z_p(S_b) \right\|^2_{\Sigma_{z,p}^{-1}}. $$

3.2 Placement-Dependent Measurements#

The placement design $s$ affects the calibration problem through the visibility graph, the projected corner locations, the corner uncertainty, and the observed measurement graph. For a candidate placement $s$, define the predicted projection

$$ \hat u_{cp}(s) = \Pi\left( K_c P_3 (T_c^{C,\mathrm{true}})^{-1} S_{\beta(p)} \bar q_p \right). $$

The image measurement model is

$$ u_{cp}(s) = \hat u_{cp}(s) + \epsilon_{cp}(s), \qquad \epsilon_{cp}(s) \sim \mathcal N(0,\Sigma_{u,cp}(s)). $$

The covariance can depend on placement:

$$ \Sigma_{u,cp}(s) = \Sigma_{u,cp} \left( \text{projected marker size}, \text{viewing angle}, \text{blur}, \text{illumination}, \text{image location} \right). $$

The observation edge set is also placement-dependent:

$$ \mathcal E(s) = \left\{ (c,p): p\text{ is visible in camera }c \text{ under placement }s \right\}. $$

Define $Z_{cp}(s)$ as the depth of marker $p$ along camera $c$’s optical axis under placement design $s$:

$$ Z_{cp}(s)= \begin{bmatrix}0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix} (T_c^{C,\mathrm{true}})^{-1} S_{\beta(p)}\bar q_p. $$

The transformation maps the board marker into homogeneous camera-frame coordinates, and the row vector selects its third coordinate. Thus $Z_{cp}(s)>0$ requires the marker to lie in front of the camera. Here $T_c^{C,\mathrm{true}}$ is the camera-to-world pose that generates the measurements; when scoring a candidate placement in practice, use the current nominal camera pose in its place.

Define $\Omega_c^{\mathrm{margin}}$ as the usable image region after excluding a border margin. Let $W_c$ and $H_c$ be the image width and height in pixels, and let $m_c\ge0$ be the required margin in pixels. With zero-based pixel coordinates $(u,v)$, where $u$ is horizontal and $v$ is vertical,

$$ \Omega_c^{\mathrm{margin}}= \left\{ (u,v)\in\mathbb R^2: \begin{aligned} m_c&\le u\le W_c-1-m_c,\\ m_c&\le v\le H_c-1-m_c \end{aligned} \right\}. $$

Membership in this region keeps the projected marker at least $m_c$ pixels inside each boundary of the pixel-coordinate rectangle.

Using these definitions, a typical deterministic visibility condition is

$$ Z_{cp}(s)>0, \qquad \hat u_{cp}(s)\in\Omega_c^{\mathrm{margin}}, \qquad a_{cp}(s)\ge a_{\min}, \qquad \alpha_{cp}(s)\le \alpha_{\max}, $$

where $a_{cp}(s)$ is projected marker size and $\alpha_{cp}(s)$ is the viewing angle between the camera ray and the board normal.

3.3 Normal Matrix and Camera-Pose Information#

For the calibration least-squares problem, the matrix used below is specifically the Gauss-Newton normal matrix. If

$$ F(x)=\frac12\|r(x)\|^2, $$

with residual vector $r(x)$ and Jacobian $J=\partial r/\partial x$, then the exact Hessian is

$$ \nabla^2 F(x) = J^\top J + \sum_i r_i(x)\nabla^2 r_i(x). $$

The normal matrix is

$$ \boxed{ H_{\mathrm{GN}}=J^\top J. } $$

This is the Gauss-Newton approximation of the Hessian. It is the right object for placement scoring for three reasons. First, after calibration, residuals are small, so the second term is usually negligible. Second, for a hypothetical candidate board, the predicted measurements are generated from the candidate itself, so the expected residual is zero and the second-order residual term vanishes in the local model. Third, with whitened Gaussian residuals, $J^\top J$ is the Fisher information matrix. Throughout the placement formulation and experiments, “Hessian” means this normal-matrix/information approximation unless stated otherwise.

What the Hessian Says About Each Component#

For a small parameter perturbation $\delta$, the second-order Taylor expansion is

$$ F(x+\delta) \approx F(x)+\nabla F(x)^\top\delta+ \frac12\delta^\top\nabla^2F(x)\delta. $$

Near a stationary point, $\nabla F(x)\approx0$, so the local loss increase is approximately

$$ \Delta F \approx \frac12\delta^\top H\delta. $$

If only parameter component $x_j$ is perturbed, then

$$ \Delta F \approx \frac12H_{jj}\delta_j^2. $$

Therefore, $H_{jj}$ measures the local curvature with respect to $x_j$ while the other components are held fixed. A large value means that the loss changes rapidly in that coordinate; a small value means that the loss is locally insensitive to it. This comparison is meaningful only after rotation, translation, and other parameter units have been scaled consistently.

The off-diagonal entry $H_{jk}$ measures coupling between components $x_j$ and $x_k$. For a joint perturbation,

$$ \Delta F \approx \frac12H_{jj}\delta_j^2+ H_{jk}\delta_j\delta_k+ \frac12H_{kk}\delta_k^2. $$

A large off-diagonal term means that the effects of the two parameters are difficult to separate, or that a change in one can be partly compensated by a change in the other. In the calibration problem, the same interpretation applies to $6\times6$ camera and board pose blocks: diagonal blocks describe local pose curvature, while off-diagonal blocks describe camera-board or camera-camera coupling.

The normal matrix also separates by measurement factor. If the whitened least-squares objective is

$$ F(x)= \sum_i\frac12\|r_i(x)\|^2, $$

then

$$ H_{\mathrm{GN}}= \sum_i H_i, \qquad H_i=J_i^\top J_i. $$

For a proposed perturbation $\delta$, factor $i$ contributes the local quadratic increase

$$ \Delta F_i \approx \frac12\delta^\top H_i\delta= \frac12\|J_i\delta\|^2. $$

This provides a direct way to measure how much a particular corner observation, camera, or board constrains a chosen parameter direction. Contributions from multiple independent factors add through $\sum_iH_i$.

The Hessian diagonal alone does not give the marginal uncertainty of a parameter because it ignores compensation through the other parameters. After gauge freedoms have been removed, the joint covariance is approximately

$$ \operatorname{Cov}(\hat x) \approx H_{\mathrm{GN}}^{-1}, $$

and $(H_{\mathrm{GN}}^{-1})_{jj}$ describes the marginal uncertainty of component $x_j$ after accounting for all parameter correlations. Thus, $H_{jj}$ is fixed-others sensitivity, whereas $(H^{-1})_{jj}$ is all-others-adjusted uncertainty.

The Hessian does not, by itself, say how much a component contributes to the current scalar loss. The residual values determine the current loss and the gradient gives its first-order change. The Hessian describes how that contribution changes locally as the parameters move.

3.4 Linearized Measurement Information#

For point $p$ on board $b$, recall its world coordinate

$$ X_p^W=R^B_bq_p+t^B_b. $$

The predicted camera-coordinate point is

$$ x_{cp} = \begin{bmatrix} X_{cp}\\Y_{cp}\\Z_{cp} \end{bmatrix} = (R^C_c)^\top \left( X_p^W-t^C_c \right). $$

The weighted reprojection residual is

$$ r_{cp} = \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} \left[ \Pi(K_cx_{cp})-u_{cp} \right]. $$

For a candidate board pose $T^B_b$, linearize the residual around the current camera and board estimates:

$$ r_{cp} \approx r_{cp}^{0} + A_{cp}\delta\eta_c + B_{cp}\delta\xi_b, $$

where

$$ A_{cp} = \frac{\partial r_{cp}}{\partial\delta\eta_c}, \qquad B_{cp} = \frac{\partial r_{cp}}{\partial\delta\xi_b}. $$

Let $J_\pi(x_{cp})=\partial\pi_{K_c}(x_{cp})/\partial x_{cp}$ denote the $2\times3$ Jacobian of the complete pixel projection with respect to the camera-frame point. It includes the focal lengths through $K_c$. For the zero-skew pinhole model,

$$ J_{\pi}(x_{cp}) = \begin{bmatrix} f_{x,c}/Z_{cp} & 0 & - f_{x,c}X_{cp}/Z_{cp}^{2} \\ 0 & f_{y,c}/Z_{cp} & - f_{y,c}Y_{cp}/Z_{cp}^{2} \end{bmatrix}. $$

Use the same left perturbations as in Section 2.3:

$$ T^C_c\leftarrow \exp(\delta\eta_c^\wedge)T^C_c, \qquad T^B_b\leftarrow \exp(\delta\xi_b^\wedge)T^B_b. $$

To derive the pose Jacobians, differentiate with respect to a six-dimensional pose perturbation. For a generic pose and a fixed point, write

$$ T=\begin{bmatrix}R&t\\0&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \bar x=\begin{bmatrix}x\\1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad y=P_3T\bar x=Rx+t. $$

Use translation-first perturbation coordinates:

$$ \delta\xi= \begin{bmatrix}\delta\rho\\\delta\phi\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \delta\xi^\wedge= \begin{bmatrix} [\delta\phi]_\times&\delta\rho\\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix}, $$

where $\delta\rho\in\mathbb R^3$ is the translational increment, $\delta\phi\in\mathbb R^3$ is the rotational increment, and $[a]_\times b=a\times b$. Because both poses map local coordinates to world coordinates, these left increments are expressed in the world frame. In particular, the first-order change in the pose’s translation is $\delta t=\delta\rho-[t]_\times\delta\phi$; $\delta\rho$ alone is not the full position change when the pose also rotates.

For the left update $T(\delta\xi)=\exp(\delta\xi^\wedge)T$, use $\exp(\delta\xi^\wedge)\approx I_4+\delta\xi^\wedge$ to obtain

$$ \begin{aligned} y(\delta\xi) &\approx y+\delta\rho+[\delta\phi]_\times y\\ &=y+\delta\rho-[y]_\times\delta\phi. \end{aligned} $$

The minus sign follows from $\delta\phi\times y=-y\times\delta\phi$. Thus,

$$ \boxed{ \left.\frac{\partial y(\delta\xi)}{\partial\delta\xi}\right|_{\delta\xi=0} =\begin{bmatrix}I_3&-[Rx+t]_\times\end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb R^{3\times6}. } $$

For comparison only, a right update $T(\delta\xi)=T\exp(\delta\xi^\wedge)$ uses increments expressed in the pose’s local frame and would give

$$ y(\delta\xi)\approx y+R\delta\rho-R[x]_\times\delta\phi, \qquad \left.\frac{\partial y(\delta\xi)}{\partial\delta\xi}\right|_{\delta\xi=0} =\begin{bmatrix}R&-R[x]_\times\end{bmatrix}. $$

These are derivatives with respect to a perturbation at the current pose, evaluated at zero increment. For the full homogeneous output $T\bar x$, append a zero row to obtain a $4\times6$ Jacobian. If rotation is ordered before translation, swap the two column blocks. The right-update formula is also given in Solà et al., A Micro Lie Theory for State Estimation in Robotics, Eq. (182).

The camera pose enters the measurement through its inverse. Holding the board pose fixed and writing $\bar x_{cp}=[x_{cp}^\top,1]^\top$, a left camera perturbation gives

$$ \begin{aligned} \bar x_{cp}(\delta\eta_c) &=\left(\exp(\delta\eta_c^\wedge)T^C_c\right)^{-1}T^B_b\bar q_p\\ &=(T^C_c)^{-1}\exp(-\delta\eta_c^\wedge)\bar X_p^W. \end{aligned} $$

With $\delta\eta_c=[\delta\rho_c^\top,\delta\phi_c^\top]^\top$, the first-order expansion is

$$ x_{cp}(\delta\eta_c) \approx x_{cp}+(R^C_c)^\top \left(-\delta\rho_c+[X_p^W]_\times\delta\phi_c\right). $$

Therefore,

$$ \left.\frac{\partial x_{cp}(\delta\eta_c)}{\partial\delta\eta_c}\right|_{\delta\eta_c=0} =(R^C_c)^\top\begin{bmatrix}-I_3&[X_p^W]_\times\end{bmatrix}. $$

Applying the chain rule through the pixel projection and the fixed whitening matrix gives the residual Jacobian.

Then the camera-pose Jacobian is

$$ \boxed{ A_{cp} = \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} J_\pi(x_{cp}) (R^C_c)^\top \begin{bmatrix} -I_3 & [X_p^W]_\times \end{bmatrix}. } $$

For the board pose, the point passes through the forward transform $T^B_b$. Its left perturbation gives

$$ X_p^W(\delta\xi_b) \approx X_p^W+\delta\rho_b-[X_p^W]_\times\delta\phi_b, \qquad \left.\frac{\partial X_p^W(\delta\xi_b)}{\partial\delta\xi_b}\right|_{\delta\xi_b=0} =\begin{bmatrix}I_3&-[X_p^W]_\times\end{bmatrix}. $$

Multiplication by $(R^C_c)^\top$ expresses that change in camera coordinates. The board-pose Jacobian is

$$ \boxed{ B_{cp} = \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} J_\pi(x_{cp}) (R^C_c)^\top \begin{bmatrix} I_3 & -[X_p^W]_\times \end{bmatrix}. } $$

For each image observation, $A_{cp}=-B_{cp}$. This is a useful consistency check: applying the same world-frame perturbation to the camera and board leaves their relative pose, and therefore the projection, unchanged. Priors can break this common-motion ambiguity by anchoring poses to the world.

These Jacobians convert a candidate physical board placement into expected camera-pose information using the same coordinates as the solver in Section 2.3. All measurement and prior Jacobians used below must be taken with respect to these left increments. Prior pose information supplied in another coordinate convention must be transformed into these coordinates before assembling the information matrix.

3.5 Marginalizing Uncertain Board Poses#

Consider all $C$ cameras and all $B$ boards jointly. Stack their world-frame left pose increments from Section 3.4 as

$$ \delta C = \begin{bmatrix} \delta\eta_1\\ \vdots\\ \delta\eta_C \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb R^{6C}, \qquad \delta B = \begin{bmatrix} \delta\xi_1\\ \vdots\\ \delta\xi_B \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb R^{6B}. $$

Introduce selection matrices for both camera and board blocks:

$$ E_c\in\mathbb R^{6\times6C}, \qquad E_b^B\in\mathbb R^{6\times6B}, \qquad E_c\delta C=\delta\eta_c, \quad E_b^B\delta B=\delta\xi_b. $$

Each selection matrix contains one $I_6$ block at the selected pose’s position and zeros elsewhere. It extracts that pose’s increment from the full parameter vector. The displayed dimensions include all poses; fixed-pose blocks are omitted when solving or evaluating covariance.

For observation $(c,p)$ on board $b=\beta(p)$, embed both local Jacobians:

$$ \boxed{ \widetilde A_{cp}=A_{cp}E_c\in\mathbb R^{2\times6C}, \qquad \widetilde B_{cp}=B_{cp}E_b^B\in\mathbb R^{2\times6B}. } $$

The residual linearization is therefore

$$ r_{cp}\approx r_{cp}^0+ \widetilde A_{cp}\delta C+ \widetilde B_{cp}\delta B. $$

For example, $\widetilde A_{cp}$ has nonzero columns only for camera $c$, and $\widetilde B_{cp}$ has nonzero columns only for board $b$. Stack all $m=|\mathcal E|$ whitened image residuals and their embedded Jacobians:

$$ r\approx r^0+J_C\delta C+J_B\delta B, \qquad J_C=\operatorname{stack}_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E}\widetilde A_{cp}, \quad J_B=\operatorname{stack}_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E}\widetilde B_{cp}. $$

Here $J_C\in\mathbb R^{2m\times6C}$ and $J_B\in\mathbb R^{2m\times6B}$. Define the joint pose increment

$$ \delta=\begin{bmatrix}\delta C\\\delta B\end{bmatrix}. $$

Let $r_{\mathrm{prior}}^0$ stack the whitened board, camera, and any coupled prior residuals at the current estimate, and let $J_{\mathrm{prior}}$ be their Jacobian with respect to $\delta$. The full local least-squares cost is

$$ \boxed{ \ell(\delta C,\delta B)= \frac12\left\|r^0+J_C\delta C+J_B\delta B\right\|^2 +\frac12\left\|r_{\mathrm{prior}}^0+J_{\mathrm{prior}}\delta\right\|^2. } $$

This is the same linearized residual model used in Section 2.3, with image residuals and prior residuals written separately and no solver damping. It approximates the nonlinear cost near the current estimate. Expanding it gives

$$ \begin{aligned} \ell(\delta C,\delta B) &=\ell_0+g^\top\delta+\frac12\delta^\top H\delta\\ &=\ell_0+g_C^\top\delta C+g_B^\top\delta B\\ &\quad+\frac12\delta C^\top H_{CC}\delta C +\delta C^\top H_{CB}\delta B +\frac12\delta B^\top H_{BB}\delta B, \end{aligned} $$

where the constant term and gradient are

$$ \ell_0=\frac12\|r^0\|^2+\frac12\|r_{\mathrm{prior}}^0\|^2, \qquad g=\begin{bmatrix}g_C\\g_B\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}J_C^\top r^0\\J_B^\top r^0\end{bmatrix} +J_{\mathrm{prior}}^\top r_{\mathrm{prior}}^0. $$

The joint information matrix is

$$ H= \begin{bmatrix} J_C^\top J_C&J_C^\top J_B\\ J_B^\top J_C&J_B^\top J_B \end{bmatrix} +H_{\mathrm{prior}} =\begin{bmatrix} H_{CC}&H_{CB}\\ H_{BC}&H_{BB} \end{bmatrix}. $$

Here $H_{\mathrm{prior}}=J_{\mathrm{prior}}^\top J_{\mathrm{prior}}$. Thus $H$ is the undamped Gauss-Newton matrix $H_{\mathrm{GN}}$ of Section 3.3. If image and prior residuals are stacked together, it is simply $J^\top J$. The quadratic form $\tfrac12\delta^\top H\delta$ acts on pose increments; residual offsets enter through $\ell_0$ and $g$.

Section 2.3 adds $\lambda D_{\mathrm{LM}}$ to this matrix when computing a damped update. That penalty is excluded from information and covariance calculations. All blocks here, including the prior terms, are evaluated at the same candidate placement and linearization point, with respect to the same left pose increments.

For camera-pose guidance, the camera poses are the quantities of interest. Board poses are auxiliary unknowns estimated jointly with them, often called nuisance variables. Their uncertainty must be included when evaluating camera calibration accuracy.

Assume the free board block $H_{BB}$ is positive definite. Hold the camera increment $\delta C$ fixed and minimize the full local cost over $\delta B$:

$$ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\delta B} =g_B+H_{BC}\delta C+H_{BB}\delta B=0. $$

Therefore,

$$ \boxed{ \delta B^*(\delta C) =-H_{BB}^{-1}\left(g_B+H_{BC}\delta C\right). } $$

These are the board motions that best fit the current residuals and the proposed camera motion in the local model. The term $-H_{BB}^{-1}g_B$ accounts for the existing residual offsets. Substituting the optimal board increments into the cost gives

$$ \begin{aligned} \ell_{\mathrm{red}}(\delta C) &=\ell\left(\delta C,\delta B^*(\delta C)\right)\\ &=\ell_0+g_C^\top\delta C+\frac12\delta C^\top H_{CC}\delta C\\ &\quad-\frac12\left(g_B+H_{BC}\delta C\right)^\top H_{BB}^{-1}\left(g_B+H_{BC}\delta C\right)\\ &=\ell_{0,\mathrm{red}}+\bar g_C^\top\delta C +\frac12\delta C^\top\Lambda_C\delta C, \end{aligned} $$

with reduced constant and gradient

$$ \ell_{0,\mathrm{red}}=\ell_0-\frac12g_B^\top H_{BB}^{-1}g_B, \qquad \bar g_C=g_C-H_{CB}H_{BB}^{-1}g_B. $$

The Hessian of this reduced cost is the Schur complement of the full board-pose block. It gives the effective information about all camera poses after eliminating all board increments:

$$ \boxed{ \Lambda_C=H_{CC}-H_{CB}H_{BB}^{-1}H_{BC}. } $$

Differentiating the reduced cost gives the camera update equation

$$ \boxed{ \Lambda_C\delta C^*=-\bar g_C =-g_C+H_{CB}H_{BB}^{-1}g_B. } $$

This is exactly the Schur-reduced normal equation in Section 2.3 with damping set to zero. The linear terms determine the update, while the quadratic term determines its information. No zero-gradient assumption is needed to obtain $\Lambda_C$.

Although the derivation above minimizes over the board increments, Gaussian marginalization gives the same information matrix. Completing the square in the full local cost yields

$$ \begin{aligned} \ell(\delta C,\delta B) &=\ell_{\mathrm{red}}(\delta C)\\ &\quad+\frac12\left(\delta B-\delta B^*(\delta C)\right)^\top H_{BB}\left(\delta B-\delta B^*(\delta C)\right). \end{aligned} $$

Integrating the corresponding Gaussian factor over all free board increments gives, when all $B$ boards are free,

$$ \begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb R^{6B}}\exp[-\ell(\delta C,\delta B)]\,d(\delta B) &\propto\exp[-\ell_{\mathrm{red}}(\delta C)]\\ &\propto\exp\left[-\bar g_C^\top\delta C -\frac12\delta C^\top\Lambda_C\delta C\right]. \end{aligned} $$

The integration contributes a factor that depends on $H_{BB}$ but not on $\delta C$. The marginalized Gaussian therefore retains both the reduced gradient $\bar g_C$ and information $\Lambda_C$. When $\Lambda_C$ is positive definite, its mean is $\delta C^*=-\Lambda_C^{-1}\bar g_C$. This equivalence applies to the fixed linearized Gaussian model used for the information calculation.

The term $H_{CC}$ is the camera information when board increments are held fixed. The subtracted term is positive semidefinite and represents the information lost because board motion can explain some of the same image changes as camera motion. After the gauge has been fixed, the predicted camera-pose covariance is approximately

$$ \boxed{\Sigma_C\approx\Lambda_C^{-1}.} $$

Here $\Sigma_C$ is the covariance of the stacked left increments $\delta C$. This inverse requires $\Lambda_C$ to be nonsingular. If gauge freedoms remain, restrict the calculation to identifiable coordinates; a pseudoinverse on that subspace does not provide absolute-pose uncertainty along unobservable directions.

The single-board expression is a special case of this joint derivation. If no factors couple different boards, then

$$ H_{BB}=\operatorname{blkdiag}_{b=1}^{B}(H_{bb}), \qquad \boxed{\Lambda_C=H_{CC}-\sum_{b=1}^{B}H_{Cb}H_{bb}^{-1}H_{bC}.} $$

Here $H_{bb}$ is the $6\times6$ information block for board $b$, and $H_{Cb}$ couples all camera increments to that board’s increment.

To identify one board’s contribution, let $\mathcal E_b=\{(c,p)\in\mathcal E:\beta(p)=b\}$ and stack only its observations:

$$ A_b=\operatorname{stack}_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E_b}(A_{cp}E_c), \qquad B_b=\operatorname{stack}_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E_b}B_{cp}. $$

Here $A_b$ still uses the full camera vector, while $B_b$ is local to the single increment $\delta\xi_b$. Its global embedding is $B_bE_b^B$, exactly as in the full system above.

If the priors separate into camera-only factors and independent board priors, let $\Lambda_b^{\mathrm{prior}}$ be board $b$’s prior information. Then

$$ H_{Cb}=A_b^\top B_b, \qquad H_{bb}=B_b^\top B_b+\Lambda_b^{\mathrm{prior}}, \qquad H_{CC}=H_{CC}^{\mathrm{prior}}+\sum_{b=1}^{B}A_b^\top A_b. $$

The effective contribution from board $b$ is therefore

$$ \boxed{ \Delta\Lambda_{C,b}(T_b^B)= A_b^\top A_b- A_b^\top B_b \left(B_b^\top B_b+\Lambda_b^{\mathrm{prior}}\right)^{-1} B_b^\top A_b, } $$

and the complete camera information is

$$ \Lambda_C=H_{CC}^{\mathrm{prior}}+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\Delta\Lambda_{C,b}(T_b^B). $$

This expression has three useful interpretations.

  1. If a board pose is accurately known, increasing its prior precision in every pose direction makes $\Delta\Lambda_{C,b}\approx A_b^\top A_b$. Almost all image information from that board contributes to the camera poses.
  2. A weaker board prior permits more board motion to compensate for camera motion, reducing the effective camera information.
  3. Without an absolute anchor, a simultaneous global transformation of all camera and board poses leaves projections unchanged. The full $\Lambda_C$ then retains unobservable absolute-pose directions.

As a scalar example, suppose a measurement observes the relative position $z=x-y+\epsilon$, where $x$ is a camera position, $y$ is a board position, and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. With an independent Gaussian board-position prior of variance $\tau^2$, the effective camera information is

$$ \Lambda_{\mathrm{scalar}} =\frac{1}{\sigma^2} -\frac{1/\sigma^4}{1/\sigma^2+1/\tau^2} =\frac{1}{\sigma^2+\tau^2}. $$

The measurement uncertainty and board-position uncertainty add, reducing the information available about the camera position.

For placement scoring, assemble the joint matrix $H(s)$ for each hypothetical multi-board placement $s$, then compute its Schur complement and camera covariance. An independent newly added board can contribute an additional $\Delta\Lambda_{C,b}$ under the separable-prior assumptions above. Moving or replacing an existing board requires updating its affected factors; its old and new contributions should not both be counted. With coupled board priors, use the full $H_{BB}$ elimination rather than the per-board sum.

If the model includes other nuisance variables, collect them with the board poses in a vector $N$ and use the same construction:

$$ \Lambda_C(s)=H_{CC}(s)-H_{CN}(s)H_{NN}(s)^{-1}H_{NC}(s), \qquad \Sigma_C(s)\approx\Lambda_C(s)^{-1}. $$

This is the next-best-pose principle: evaluate the expected measurements of a hypothetical placement, account for all nuisance uncertainty, and select the placement that gives the largest camera-uncertainty reduction.

3.6 Upper-Level Camera-Uncertainty Objective#

The upper-level problem chooses $s$ so that the lower-level camera poses are accurate and well conditioned. Optimized reprojection residual is not the best design objective, because a weak or degenerate layout can still fit its own noisy observations. A more useful objective is camera-pose uncertainty.

Linearize the lower-level residuals at the nominal solution and split the perturbation into camera-pose variables and nuisance variables:

$$ \delta x = \begin{bmatrix} \theta\\ \zeta \end{bmatrix}, $$

where $\theta=\delta C$ contains camera-pose perturbations and $\zeta=\delta B$ contains board-pose perturbations from Section 3.5, both using world-frame left increments; additional nuisance variables can also be included in $\zeta$. The Gauss-Newton or Fisher information matrix is

$$ H(s) = J(s)^\top W(s)J(s) + H_{\mathrm{prior}}(s). $$

Here $J(s)$ stacks the unwhitened image-residual Jacobians and $W(s)$ contains their measurement precisions. Equivalently, use the whitened Jacobians of Section 3.5 and omit $W(s)$. Partition the information matrix as

$$ H(s) = \begin{bmatrix} H_{\theta\theta}(s) & H_{\theta\zeta}(s)\\ H_{\zeta\theta}(s) & H_{\zeta\zeta}(s) \end{bmatrix}. $$

After marginalizing the board poses and other nuisance variables, the effective camera-pose information is the Schur complement:

$$ \Lambda_\theta(s) = H_{\theta\theta}(s) - H_{\theta\zeta}(s) H_{\zeta\zeta}(s)^{-1} H_{\zeta\theta}(s). $$

The predicted camera-pose covariance is

$$ \Sigma_\theta(s) \approx \Lambda_\theta(s)^{-1}. $$

The A-optimal board-placement problem is therefore

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min} &\quad \operatorname{tr} \left( D\Lambda_\theta(s)^{-1}D^\top \right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s) \\ \text{s.t.} &\quad x^\star(s) \in \underset{x}{\arg\min}\;\mathcal L(x;s), \\ &\quad \Lambda_\theta(s) = H_{\theta\theta}(s) - H_{\theta\zeta}(s)H_{\zeta\zeta}(s)^{-1}H_{\zeta\theta}(s), \\ &\quad s\in\mathcal S. \end{aligned} } \tag{BL-A} $$

The matrix $D$ maps the left pose increments to the errors being scored and normalizes their units. If the tolerances concern camera-center position and orientation, first use the position-change relation from Section 3.4:

$$ \begin{bmatrix}\delta t_c^C\\\delta\phi_c\end{bmatrix} =G_c\delta\eta_c, \qquad G_c=\begin{bmatrix} I_3&-[t_c^C]_\times\\ 0&I_3 \end{bmatrix}. $$

For position tolerance $\sigma_t$ in metres and angular tolerance $\sigma_\phi$ in radians, choose

$$ D=\operatorname{blkdiag}_{c=1}^{C} \left( \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_t^{-1}I_3&0\\ 0&\sigma_\phi^{-1}I_3 \end{bmatrix}G_c \right). $$

Evaluate $G_c$ at the nominal camera pose and omit fixed-camera blocks. Then $D\Sigma_\theta D^\top$ is the normalized covariance of camera-center position and world-frame orientation increments. Direct diagonal scaling without $G_c$ would instead score the left tangent’s translation component $\delta\rho_c$. At $T_c^C=I$, as in Section 4.1, $G_c=I_6$ and the two coincide.

The feasible set $\mathcal S$ contains the physical and production constraints:

$$ \mathcal S = \left\{ s=\{S_b\}_{b=1}^B: g_{\mathrm{phys}}(s)\le 0,\; g_{\mathrm{vis}}(s)\le 0,\; g_{\mathrm{quality}}(s)\le 0,\; g_{\mathrm{time}}(s)\le 0 \right\}. $$

Typical constraints are board mountable regions, collision avoidance with the vehicle, minimum spacing between boards, image-bound margins, minimum visible marker count, acceptable viewing angle, acceptable projected marker size, and takt-time or robot-motion limits.

3.7 Feasible Physical Placements#

The upper-level feasible set should encode physical, visibility, quality, and production constraints:

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal S = \left\{ s=\{S_b\}_{b=1}^B: g_{\mathrm{phys}}(s)\le 0,\; g_{\mathrm{vis}}(s)\le 0,\; g_{\mathrm{quality}}(s)\le 0,\; g_{\mathrm{time}}(s)\le 0 \right\}. } $$

Typical physical constraints include

$$ S_b\in SE(3), \qquad t_b\in\mathcal W_{\mathrm{factory}}, \qquad R_b e_3\cdot n_{\mathrm{floor/wall}} \ge \gamma_{\mathrm{mount}}, $$$$ \operatorname{dist}(S_b,S_{b'}) \ge d_{\min}, \qquad \operatorname{collision}(S_b,\text{vehicle})=0. $$

Typical visibility and quality constraints include

$$ Z_{cp}(s)>0, \qquad \hat u_{cp}(s)\in\Omega_c^{\mathrm{margin}}, \qquad N_{cb}^{\mathrm{visible}}(s)\ge N_{\min}, $$$$ a_{\min}\le a_{cp}(s)\le a_{\max}, \qquad \alpha_{cp}(s)\le \alpha_{\max}. $$

For an EOL station, takt-time or robot-motion constraints can be added as

$$ C_{\mathrm{move}}(s)\le C_{\max}. $$

Corner-Quality and Detection Constraints#

Pure geometric information can favor an excessively oblique board. In practice, strong foreshortening makes corners elongated, blurred, or difficult to detect. Candidate scoring should therefore use pose-dependent image covariance,

$$ \Sigma_{u,cp} = \Sigma_{u,cp}(T^B_b), $$

or an expected detection probability:

$$ \Delta H(T^B_b) = \sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E(T^B_b)} p_{\mathrm{det},cp}(T^B_b) J_{cp}^{\top} \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1}(T^B_b) J_{cp}. $$

This balances geometric strength against actual feature-localization accuracy. Otherwise, the placement optimizer can prefer grazing views that look informative geometrically but produce anisotropic and unreliable corner measurements.

The feasible set $\mathcal F$ should therefore require:

$$ Z_{cp}>0, $$

enough visible markers, image points inside the image with a safety margin, sufficient projected square size, acceptable focus and illumination, and a non-grazing incidence angle.

3.8 Continuous Board-Pose Optimization#

For continuous placement variables, optimize the uncertainty objective subject to the feasible set, using derivatives of the predicted information matrix. A numerical iteration proposes a new board pose; it does not require collecting an image or physically moving the board at every step. The first experiment instantiates this formulation with SLSQP.

The derivative below applies locally where the measurement model is differentiable and the visibility set is fixed. Visibility changes and geometric constraints must also be handled by the placement optimizer.

For the uncertainty objective

$$ \mathcal U(s) = \operatorname{tr} \left( D\Lambda_\theta(s)^{-1}D^\top \right), $$

the differential is

$$ \boxed{ d\mathcal U = - \operatorname{tr} \left[ \Lambda_\theta^{-1} D^\top D \Lambda_\theta^{-1} d\Lambda_\theta \right]. } $$

The Schur-complement differential is

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} d\Lambda_\theta &= dH_{\theta\theta} - dH_{\theta\zeta}H_{\zeta\zeta}^{-1}H_{\zeta\theta} \\ &\quad + H_{\theta\zeta}H_{\zeta\zeta}^{-1} dH_{\zeta\zeta} H_{\zeta\zeta}^{-1}H_{\zeta\theta} \\ &\quad - H_{\theta\zeta}H_{\zeta\zeta}^{-1} dH_{\zeta\theta}. \end{aligned} } $$

This gives a path to gradient-based board-placement optimization when the placement variables are continuous.

3.9 Sequential Next-Board Placement#

If boards are placed one at a time, the upper level becomes a next-best-placement problem. Suppose the current calibration data provide camera-pose information

$$ \Lambda_\theta^{\mathrm{cur}}. $$

A candidate new board placement $s_{\mathrm{new}}$ contributes expected information

$$ \Delta\Lambda_\theta(s_{\mathrm{new}}). $$

Then choose

$$ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} s_{\mathrm{new}}^\star = \underset{s_{\mathrm{new}}\in\mathcal S_{\mathrm{new}}}{\arg\min} \\ \left\{ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{tr} \left[ D \left( \Lambda_\theta^{\mathrm{cur}} + \Delta\Lambda_\theta(s_{\mathrm{new}}) \right)^{-1} D^\top \right] \\ &\quad+\lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s_{\mathrm{new}}) \end{aligned} \right\}. \end{gathered} } \tag{NBP} $$

For $M$ future placements, use

$$ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} \{s_m^\star\}_{m=1}^M = \underset{\{s_m\}_{m=1}^M\subset\mathcal S}{\arg\min} \\ \left\{ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{tr} \left[ D \left( \Lambda_\theta^{\mathrm{cur}} + \sum_{m=1}^M \Delta\Lambda_\theta(s_m) \right)^{-1} D^\top \right] \\ &\quad+\lambda_{\mathrm{cost}} \sum_{m=1}^M C_{\mathrm{place}}(s_m) \end{aligned} \right\}. \end{gathered} } \tag{NBP-M} $$

This formulation directly answers the operational question: where should the next board be placed to reduce camera-pose uncertainty the most?

Interpreting the Information Geometrically#

The information matrix translates into concrete board-placement guidance:

Board-placement propertyEffect on pose estimation
Large board footprint in the imageThe $1/Z$ terms in $J_\pi$ increase, and widely separated points provide stronger rotational leverage.

Therefore, repeatedly placing a small, fronto-parallel board at the image center is usually inefficient. It generates nearly redundant Jacobian rows. A better sequence contains:

  1. A large, well-centered observation for robust initialization.
  2. A laterally displaced observation with tilt about one board axis.
  3. An observation on the opposite side with tilt about the other axis.
  4. A different board-to-camera distance.
  5. For multi-camera calibration, placements in camera-overlap regions so the same board points are observed by multiple cameras.

The exact next placement should be determined by the weakest eigenvector of the current marginalized information matrix. For example, if the largest covariance direction is mostly a combination of camera $t_z$ and pitch, the next-best score will usually favor a laterally displaced and tilted board rather than another centered, fronto-parallel observation.

Practical Placement-Optimization Loop#

The resulting EOL procedure is:

  1. Solve centralized problem (P) using all measurements currently available.
  2. Compute the current sparse normal matrix and camera-pose covariance.
  3. Parameterize the board’s continuous position and orientation and define workspace, visibility, and corner-quality constraints.
  4. At each numerical trial pose, predict visibility, pixel positions, corner covariance, and reprojection Jacobians.
  5. For an additional observation, add its predicted information to the current normal matrix; for a replacement, rebuild the information for the revised layout without double-counting the old observation.
  6. Marginalize board poses and other nuisance variables with the Schur complement.
  7. Optimize the normalized camera-pose uncertainty over the feasible pose variables. If the station allows only a discrete set of mounts, solve the corresponding candidate-selection problem instead.
  8. Place and survey the board, capture the observations, resolve (P), and repeat.

The central idea is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{The loss estimates the poses; its marginalized curvature tells us where to place the board.} } $$

3.10 Complete Board-Placement Formulation#

For the method statement, the most useful final form is

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min} &\quad \operatorname{tr} \left( D \Lambda_\theta(s)^{-1} D^\top \right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s) \\ \text{s.t.} &\quad x^\star(s) = \underset{x}{\arg\min} \; \mathcal L(x;s), \\ &\quad \Lambda_\theta(s) = H_{\theta\theta}(s) - H_{\theta\zeta}(s)H_{\zeta\zeta}(s)^{-1}H_{\zeta\theta}(s), \\ &\quad H(s) = J(s)^\top W(s)J(s) + H_{\mathrm{prior}}(s), \\ &\quad s\in\mathcal S. \end{aligned} } \tag{BL-final} $$

In words, the lower level estimates camera and board poses, while the upper level chooses physical board placements that make the lower-level camera-pose estimate maximally informative. The final objective should not merely minimize the lower-level reprojection error. It should minimize the predicted camera-pose uncertainty induced by that lower-level optimization.

The experiments now instantiate this formulation with increasing system complexity. Alternative criteria, residual-based objectives, and the fixed-budget discrete subset formulation are retained in Appendix C.

4.1 One Camera, One Board: Continuous Pose Optimization#

Start with one fixed camera and one movable board. The task is to optimize the board’s position and orientation so that a subsequent image estimates the camera’s six-degree-of-freedom extrinsic pose as accurately as possible. The camera intrinsics are already known. We start from one board pose and use continuous constrained optimization; we do not choose among a few manually prescribed placements.

The board’s world pose must be known after moving it, for example from an accurately surveyed fixture. In this first example, that pose is treated as exact. If both the camera and board poses were completely unknown, an image would determine only their relative pose, not the camera’s absolute world pose. Finite board-survey uncertainty is handled by the marginalization in Section 3.5; this first experiment isolates the exactly known-board case.

Geometry and Design Variables#

Use the nominal camera frame as the world frame: $x$ points right, $y$ down, and $z$ forward. The fixed camera has a $1280\times960$ image, $f_x=f_y=800$ pixels, principal point $(640,480)$, and no distortion. The board has $7\times5$ corners with $0.10$ m spacing, spanning $0.60\times0.40$ m.

The simulation’s true camera pose equals the nominal pose. In a real station, evaluate the design using the current approximate camera pose; the purpose of the new image is to refine that estimate.

The six continuous design variables are

$$ s=(x_B,y_B,z_B,\alpha,\beta,\gamma). $$

Translation is in metres and the reported angles are in degrees. Define the board-to-world rotation and world corner positions by

$$ R_B(s)= R_z(\gamma)R_y(\beta)R_x(\alpha) \operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1), \qquad X_i(s)=R_B(s)q_i+t_B. $$

At zero angles the printed board normal faces the camera. These are fixed-axis $x,y,z$ Euler angles, matching SciPy’s lowercase xyz convention. The initial board pose is

$$ s_0=(0.25,\;0.15,\;2.50,\;5^\circ,\;8^\circ,\;0^\circ). $$

Turn Camera Uncertainty into the Placement Objective#

For each proposed board pose, predict the corner locations and the camera-pose Jacobian. At the nominal camera pose $T_C=I$, use camera perturbations ordered as $\delta\theta=(\delta t,\delta\omega)$, with translation in metres and rotation in radians:

$$ J_i(s)= \frac{1}{\sigma_i(s)} J_\pi(X_i(s)) \begin{bmatrix}-I &[X_i(s)]_\times\end{bmatrix}, \qquad J(s)=\operatorname{stack}_i J_i(s). $$

Here $J_\pi(X)=\partial\pi_K(X)/\partial X$ differentiates the complete pixel projection defined in Section 1.3, including the focal lengths, and $[X]_\times v=X\times v$. The factor $1/\sigma_i$ whitens the two pixel-coordinate residuals for corner $i$.

To represent poorer corner localization at grazing angles, assume the illustrative noise model

$$ \nu_i(s)=n_B(s)^\top\frac{-X_i(s)}{\|X_i(s)\|}, \qquad \sigma_i(s)=\frac{0.25}{\nu_i(s)}\ \mathrm{px}. $$

Here $n_B=R_B(0,0,1)^\top$ is the printed board normal. The feasibility constraints below keep $\nu_i$ positive and away from zero. This incidence-dependent noise is an assumed model, not a measured property of a particular camera. For camera-pose estimation, its values are fixed at the nominal design geometry.

With independent Gaussian image noise and a full-rank whitened Jacobian, the local camera-pose covariance is approximately

$$ H(s)=J(s)^\top J(s), \qquad C(s)=H(s)^{-1}. $$

This is a local, linearized covariance prediction, not a guarantee of global pose-estimation accuracy; see the Ceres covariance documentation.

Normalize translation by a $5$ mm tolerance and rotation by a $0.1^\circ$ tolerance:

$$ D= \operatorname{diag} \left( \frac{1}{0.005}, \frac{1}{0.005}, \frac{1}{0.005}, \frac{1}{\pi/1800}, \frac{1}{\pi/1800}, \frac{1}{\pi/1800} \right). $$

The board-placement optimization is

$$ \boxed{ s^\star= \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min}\; U(s), \qquad U(s)=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(D\,C(s)\,D^\top\right). } $$

Thus we minimize over the physical board pose $s$, not the camera pose. The objective is the predicted sum of squared, tolerance-normalized camera-pose errors. The tolerance ratio specifies the relative importance of translation and rotation.

The feasible set $\mathcal S$ requires:

  • Board centre: $x_B\in[-0.6,0.6]$ m, $y_B\in[-0.4,0.4]$ m, and $z_B\in[1,3]$ m.
  • Board angles: $\alpha,\beta\in[-60^\circ,60^\circ]$ and $\gamma\in[-45^\circ,45^\circ]$.
  • Every corner remains at least $40$ pixels inside the image and at least $0.6$ m in front of the camera.
  • Every corner’s incidence angle is at most $70^\circ$, and adjacent grid corners remain at least $12$ pixels apart.

How the Optimizer Decides Which Way to Move#

Moving the board changes $X_i$, the noise weights, and therefore $J$ and $H$. The derivative of the uncertainty objective with respect to board coordinate $s_j$ is

$$ \frac{\partial U}{\partial s_j}= -\operatorname{tr} \left( D H^{-1} \frac{\partial H}{\partial s_j} H^{-1}D^\top \right). $$

This derivative tells us whether increasing a board coordinate improves or worsens predicted camera precision. It accounts for both projection geometry and the change in corner noise.

The implementation uses SciPy’s SLSQP constrained optimizer with numerical derivatives, starting only at $s_0$. At each iteration it evaluates $U$ and the constraints near the current pose, builds a local quadratic subproblem, and updates the six board coordinates together. Coordinates are scaled internally so that metres and degrees have comparable numerical ranges.

In schematic form, with inequality constraints $c(s)\ge0$, the update is

$$ \begin{aligned} d_k &= \underset{d}{\arg\min}\; \nabla U(s_k)^\top d+\frac12d^\top B_kd,\\ &\text{subject to }\; c(s_k)+\nabla c(s_k)d\ge0 \text{ and the variable bounds},\\ s_{k+1}&=s_k+a_kd_k. \end{aligned} $$

Here $B_k$ approximates the Hessian of the constrained optimization Lagrangian, and $a_k$ is a line-search step length. This calculation produces a new continuous pose; it is not a lookup in a candidate-pose grid. All of these evaluations are numerical predictions, so the board does not need to be physically moved at every optimizer iteration.

Why Not Place the Board Symmetrically at the Image Centre?#

A centred board is an intuitive choice, but visual symmetry does not automatically minimize the uncertainty of all six camera-pose parameters. The off-centre result needs a precise interpretation: the centre is competitive, and the lower-left corner is not uniquely optimal.

To check the effect of centring, fix the physical board centre on the optical axis, $x_B=y_B=0$, and continuously optimize the remaining four coordinates:

$$ \underset{z_B,\alpha,\beta,\gamma}{\operatorname{minimize}}\; U(0,0,z_B,\alpha,\beta,\gamma), \qquad (0,0,z_B,\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathcal S. $$

This uses the same objective, noise model, and feasibility constraints as the six-coordinate optimization. SLSQP starts from the previous solution’s depth and orientation, with its lateral position reset to zero. It converges in 16 iterations to a local centred solution with $z_B=1$ m and approximately $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(0^\circ,49.57^\circ,-10.52^\circ)$. Thus, “centred” does not mean “front-facing”: the board centre projects to the principal point, but its orientation remains free.

The off-centre solution sacrifices some translation precision for better rotation precision, and the chosen $5$ mm / $0.1^\circ$ normalization favors that trade-off. The earlier 98.9% reduction is relative to the distant initial pose and combines changes in depth, orientation, and lateral position; it is not a 98.9% advantage of off-centre placement over centred placement.

This is a diagnostic constrained optimization, not a selection among manually prescribed experiments. Both reported solutions are local numerical solutions, not proofs of global optimality.

The geometric reason is that different camera motions can produce similar image motions. For a camera-frame point $(X,Y,Z)$, the unwhitened horizontal pixel coordinate has the following derivatives under the camera-perturbation convention used above:

$$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t_x}=-\frac{f}{Z}, \qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial\omega_y}= -f\left(1+\frac{X^2}{Z^2}\right). $$

Here $t_x$ and $\omega_y$ refer to camera translation and rotation, not board-design coordinates. For a nearly front-facing board near the image centre, $X/Z$ is small and corner depths are similar. Both a sideways camera translation and a small rotation about the camera’s $y$ axis then produce approximately uniform horizontal image shifts. Their Jacobian columns are nearly proportional, making those camera parameters difficult to distinguish.

Tilting the board introduces depth variation across corners, while moving it off-axis changes the spatial pattern of rotational image motion. Either can help separate the effects of different pose parameters. Symmetry can reduce some parameter correlations, but does not eliminate this translation-rotation coupling. The objective accounts for the joint correlations through $C=(J^\top J)^{-1}$, rather than scoring only the diagonal of $J^\top J$. These are local covariance predictions under the assumed measurement noise, not measured real-camera accuracies; see the Ceres covariance documentation.

Symmetry also does not imply that the minimum must occur at the centre. In this model, reflecting both the board position and orientation horizontally or vertically preserves the score. All four mirrored versions of the off-centre solution were checked: they satisfy the constraints and have the same $U=0.42765855$. The lower-left placement is therefore one member of a symmetry-related set, not a uniquely preferred image region.

For the fixed-axis xyz Euler convention used here, horizontal reflection changes the signs of x, beta, and gamma; vertical reflection changes the signs of y, alpha, and gamma. Applying both gives the fourth pose. Reflecting only the board centre while retaining its original orientation is not this symmetry check.

Finally, the model assumes exact intrinsics, no lens distortion, and an exactly known board pose. Its noise model penalizes grazing incidence but includes no additional penalty for peripheral-image errors. Uncertainty in intrinsic calibration, residual distortion, actual corner-detection quality, and board surveying can change the optimum. The conclusion is a model-specific translation-versus-rotation trade-off, not a general recommendation to move calibration boards to image corners.

$$ \widehat T_C= \underset{T_C}{\arg\min}\; \frac12\sum_i \left\| \frac{ \pi_K\!\left(R_C^\top[X_i(s^\star)-t_C]\right)-u_i }{\sigma_i(s^\star)} \right\|^2. $$

Corner detection often becomes challenging on original images due to severe distortion or extreme viewing angles. To address this limitation, we can create a virtual camera with an optimized viewpoint. This transformation adjusts the perspective so that the chessboard appears as if viewed head-on, significantly improving corner detection reliability. We can then accurately detect the corners on this warped image rather than struggling with the difficult perspective of the original.

Building on the one-camera placement optimization in Section 4.1, consider a calibration station with two cameras and two physical calibration boards. Here the lower level estimates both camera poses and both uncertain board poses from noisy image corners and noisy surveyed board corners. This separate experiment uses a discrete candidate set to illustrate several upper-level search strategies; Section 4.1 demonstrates direct continuous board-pose optimization from one starting pose.

A candidate is feasible only if both cameras see at least 20 corners, the projected corners are inside the image margin, the projected marker size is large enough, and the board is not too grazing.

Noise Model#

The simulated image noise follows the placement-dependent idea from the bilevel formulation. For each visible corner, the pixel standard deviation is

$$ \sigma_{u,cp} = \operatorname{clip} \left( 0.28 \left[ 1 + 0.35\left(\frac{18}{a_{cp}}\right)^2 + 0.16\left(\frac{1}{\max(\iota_{cp},0.10)}-1\right) + 0.30\rho_{cp}^2 \right], 0.25, 3.5 \right)\ \mathrm{px}. $$

Here $a_{cp}$ is the projected marker size in pixels, $\iota_{cp}$ is the incidence between the board normal and the camera ray, and $\rho_{cp}$ is normalized distance from the image center. This makes small, oblique, or edge-of-image corners noisier. Surveyed world marker positions use $\sigma_z=2.5$ mm.

Upper-Level Solvers#

For each feasible candidate board pose $g$, the script predicts visibility and computes the Schur-marginalized camera-pose information contribution $\Delta\Lambda_g$. For a selected two-board set $\mathcal A=\{g_1,g_2\}$, the A-optimal score is

$$ U(\mathcal A) = \operatorname{tr} \left[ D \left( \sum_{g\in\mathcal A}\Delta\Lambda_g \right)^{-1} D^\top \right]. $$

The simulation compares four placement strategies:

  1. Manual initial: two nearby, front-workspace boards.
  2. Greedy next-best: choose the first board with the best single-board score, then choose the second board that gives the best updated two-board score.
  3. Simulated annealing: start from the manual pair, randomly replace one board at a time, and accept or reject moves using a decreasing temperature.
  4. Exhaustive optimum: enumerate all valid two-board pairs in the discrete candidate set and choose the pair with the lowest A-optimal trace.

This mirrors the reference strategies in the placement-guidance literature: sample a feasible pose set, reject degenerate candidates, score by parameter uncertainty rather than residual alone, and use either direct search, sequential next-best selection, or simulated annealing to search the pose space.

Jacobian-Guided Refinement of the 40-Board Layout#

The 40-board uniform layout can also be treated as the starting point for the board-placement guidance method. Here the goal is not to add one more board, but to replace the 40 boards with a better 40-board set using the solved uniform layout as the current calibration state.

The adjustment loop is:

  1. Solve the original uniform 40-board layout with centralized BA.
  2. Build the Schur-marginalized camera-pose information matrix from that solved state.
  3. Generate the same perimeter-ring candidate board poses used by the vehicle experiment.
  4. For each candidate, predict visible marker observations using the solved camera poses, build the candidate Jacobian, marginalize the candidate board pose, and score the candidate by camera covariance reduction.
  5. Start from the exact 40 initial board positions and evaluate one-for-one swaps: remove one existing board, add one candidate, and keep the swap only if the predicted camera covariance improves.
  6. Repeat the swap search until no candidate swap improves the Schur-marginalized camera-pose trace.
  7. Re-simulate noisy image and surveyed-world measurements for the refined board placement, then solve the calibration again.

For one candidate board pose $g$, the method linearizes the image residuals at the solved camera poses from the initial 40-board layout. Its camera-pose information contribution is computed after marginalizing the candidate board pose:

$$ \Lambda_g = J_c^\top W J_c - J_c^\top W J_b \left(J_b^\top W J_b\right)^{-1} J_b^\top W J_c . $$

For a one-for-one replacement, the predicted score is the reduction in the normalized camera covariance trace after replacing an existing board contribution $\Lambda_r$ with $\Lambda_g$. This means the method is not just counting visible corners. It asks whether the new board’s Jacobian directions reduce the weak camera-pose modes left by the solved uniform layout.

4.6 What the Experiments Establish#

The one-board experiment demonstrates continuous physical pose optimization and exposes the translation-rotation trade-off behind an off-centre result. The two-camera experiments add shared geometry and uncertain board poses. Appendix A. Geometric Preprocessing and Board Survey Refinement#

These derivations support initialization and the fixed-board image-processing experiment in Section 4.2. Ray-plane reconstruction is an optional geometric route, not a required residual in the reprojection-based solver of Section 2.

A.1 Optional Geometric Pipeline#

  1. Do single camera intrinsic calibration

  2. Measure each corner in the world coordinate

  3. Refine the corners’ coordinates according to board constraints (plane, parallel, equally spaced)

  4. Find the plane equation in the world coordinate

  5. Initialize each camera’s extrinsic parameters by solving the perspective-n-point (PnP) problem

  6. Estimate the 3D coordinates by the intersection of the image ray and board plane

  7. Construct the objective loss function from pixel reprojection residuals that share board-generated marker points, plus optional world-point or pose priors

A.2 Refine Surveyed Corners Using Board Constraints#

Assume the center of the board is located at the origin of a local coordinate, $(0,0,0)^\top$, the corner coordinate is located at $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}^N_{i=1}$, then the board normal is $(0,0,1)^\top$. Assume the board is transformed to the world coordinate by rotation, $\mathbf{R}$, and translation, $\mathbf{t}$. We have the observed coordinates $\{\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i\}$. Then we minimize the objective:

$$ (\mathbf{R}^*, \mathbf{t}^*) = \underset{\mathbf{R}\in SO(3),\,\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb R^3}{\arg\min} \sum_i \left\| \hat{\mathbf{y}}_i-(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}_i+\mathbf{t}) \right\|^2, \qquad \mathbf{y}_i^*=\mathbf{R}^*\mathbf{x}_i+\mathbf{t}^*. $$

Because the board-corner correspondences are already known, this is an orthogonal Procrustes/Kabsch rigid-alignment problem. ICP is needed only when the correspondences also have to be estimated.

If the board-frame origin is at the board center, then the center of the board is $\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{t}^*$, and the normal of the board is $\mathbf{R}^*[0, 0, 1]^\top$.

Now we have refined board corners in the world coordinates, i.e., $\{\mathbf{y}_i^*\}^N_{i=1}$.

Unknown Board Dimension#

If we do not have the board dimension, then we can directly fit the plane using the measured corners, $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}^N_{i=1}$ (with a slight abuse of notation). Use the plane convention $\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x} + c = 0$ (with unit normal vector, i.e., $\|\mathbf{n}\| = 1$). Then we can solve for $\mathbf{n}$ and $c$ by minimizing the following objective:

$$ \underset{\mathbf{n}, c}{\text{argmin}} \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \|\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + c\|^2 \\ \text{s.t. } \|\mathbf{n}\| = 1 $$

For any fixed $\mathbf{n}$, taking derivative with respect to $c$ and setting it to zero, we have:

$$ c = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_i \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x}_i = - \mathbf{n}^\top \bar{\mathbf{x}} $$

where $\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_i \mathbf{x}_i$ is the mean of the measured corners.

So, the objective function becomes:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \|\mathbf{n}^\top (\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})\|^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{N}\mathbf{n}^\top \left(\sum_i (\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top\right) \mathbf{n} \\ & = \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{\Sigma}_x \mathbf{n} \end{aligned} $$

where $\mathbf{\Sigma}_x = \frac{1}{N} \sum_i (\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top$ is the biased sample covariance matrix of the measured corners.

Considering $\|\mathbf{n}\| = 1$, $\mathbf{n}$ is the eigenvector of $\mathbf{\Sigma}_x$ corresponding to its smallest eigenvalue.

The refined corners’ coordinates are the projection of the measured corners on the plane, which can be written as:

$$ \mathbf{y}_i = \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{n}^\top (\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{x}_i - (\mathbf{n}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + c)\mathbf{n}. $$

which has clear geometric meaning: the measured corners minus the signed distance along the plane normal. The sign of $\mathbf n$ remains arbitrary as long as $c$ is changed consistently.

Appendix B. Ray-Plane Geometry and Optional Residuals#

The first two derivations use world-to-camera extrinsics $(R,t)$: $X^C=RX^W+t$. Relative to the main article’s camera-to-world pose, $R=(R^C)^\top$ and $t=-(R^C)^\top t^C$. The coordinate conversion is explicit here so that the main formulation retains one consistent local-to-world convention.

B.1 Closed-Form Ray-Plane Intersection#

We are given a 3D plane:

$$\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x} + c = 0,$$

and a camera model with the projection equation:

$$d\, \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{K} (\mathbf{R}\, \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{t}),$$

where:

  • $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the homogeneous image coordinate (usually $\mathbf{u} = [u, v, 1]^\top$)
  • $d$ is an unknown scale (depth) factor
  • $\mathbf{K}$ is the camera intrinsic matrix
  • $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{t}$ are the rotation and translation (extrinsic parameters) of the camera
  • $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the 3D point we wish to recover

Our goal is to find $\mathbf{x}$ in terms of the known quantities $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t}, \mathbf{n},$ and $c$.

Rewrite the Projection Equation

$$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{R}^\top \left(d\,\mathbf{K}^{-1} \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{t}\right).$$

However, the scale factor $d$ is still unknown.

Use the Plane Constraint to Solve for $d$

Since $\mathbf{x}$ lies on the plane, it must satisfy:

$$\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x} + c = 0.$$

Substitute the expression we just found for $\mathbf{x}$:

$$\mathbf{n}^\top \Bigl(\mathbf{R}^\top (d\,\mathbf{K}^{-1} \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{t})\Bigr) + c = 0.$$

Expanding the scalar equation gives

$$ d\,\mathbf{n}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{u}- \mathbf{n}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{t} + c = 0. $$

Now solve for $d$:

$$d = \frac{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{t} - c}{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{K}^{-1} \mathbf{u}}.$$

Note: It is important that $\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{K}^{-1} \mathbf{u} \neq 0$ so that the division is valid (this means that the back-projected ray is not parallel to the plane).

Substitute the expression for $d$ back into the equation for $\mathbf{x}$:

$$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{R}^\top \left( \frac{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{t} - c}{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{K}^{-1} \mathbf{u}}\, \mathbf{K}^{-1} \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{t} \right).$$

B.2 Ray-Plane Intersection as an Optimization Problem#

If we write the ray-plane intersection as an optimization, the pixel-space objective should be dimensionally consistent:

$$ \min_{\mathbf{x}} \left\| \mathbf{u}- \Pi\left( \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}) \right) \right\|^2 \tag{1} $$

subject to the plane constraint

$$ \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x} + c = 0, $$

where $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb R^2$ is a pixel coordinate and $\Pi$ is the perspective-division operator from Section 1.3:

$$ \Pi([a,b,d]^\top)= \begin{bmatrix} a/d\\ b/d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad d\ne0. $$

If $\mathbf{u}$ instead denotes normalized undistorted image coordinates, then $\mathbf{K}$ can be omitted and the ray direction is $\mathbf{R}^{-1}[\mathbf{u}^\top,1]^\top$.

Even when the measured image coordinate $\mathbf{u}$ is noisy, the pinhole camera model combined with the infinite plane constraint induces a homography from the plane to the image. For any image coordinate, there is a back-projected ray, and if that ray intersects the plane, the corresponding 3D point projects exactly back to the same coordinate. Therefore, Eq. (1) has minimum value zero whenever the ray intersects the plane. By itself, ray-plane intersection does not correct noisy image measurements; board-marker geometry or other multi-view observations must provide the denoising constraint.

  1. Parameterizing the Back‐Projection Ray

For normalized image coordinates, any 3D point $\mathbf{x}$ whose projection is $\mathbf{u}$ must satisfy

$$ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}= \lambda \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u}\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Solving for $\mathbf{x}$ gives

$$ \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{R}^{-1} \Bigl( \lambda \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} - \mathbf{t} \Bigr). $$

It is convenient to define the camera center in world coordinates as

$$ \mathbf{x}_c = -\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t}, $$

and to introduce the direction vector

$$ \mathbf{d} = \mathbf{R}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Thus, the 3D point $\mathbf{x}$ can be written as

$$ \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_c + \lambda \mathbf{d}. $$

All points of this form project exactly to $\mathbf{u}$, which means the optimal value of Eq. (1) is zero

  1. Enforcing the Plane Constraint

Since $\mathbf{x}$ must lie on the plane, we impose

$$ \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x} + c = 0. $$

Substitute $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_c + \lambda\mathbf{d}$ into the plane equation:

$$ \mathbf{n}^\top \Bigl( \mathbf{x}_c + \lambda\mathbf{d} \Bigr) + c = 0. $$

Expanding, we obtain

$$ \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x}_c + \lambda \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{d} + c = 0. $$

Recalling that $\mathbf{x}_c = -\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t}$, the equation becomes

$$ - \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} + \lambda \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{d} + c = 0. $$

Solving for $\lambda$, we have

$$ \lambda \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{d} = \mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} - c, $$

or equivalently,

$$ \lambda = \frac{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} - c}{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{d}} = \frac{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} - c}{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}}, $$

provided that $\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{d} \neq 0$.

A physically visible intersection also requires positive camera depth.

  1. The Final Solution

Substitute the value of $\lambda$ back into the expression for $\mathbf{x}$:

$$ \mathbf{x} = -\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} + \frac{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} - c}{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}} \mathbf{R}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

This is the unique 3D point on the plane $\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x} + c = 0$ selected by the measured ray, provided the ray is not parallel to the plane and the depth is positive.

  1. Summary

a. Back‐Projection: The projection model

$$ \mathbf{x} \mapsto \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{t}}{\mathbf{e}^\top (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{t})} $$

is a homography when restricted to the plane. Hence, every point along the ray

$$ \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{u}) = \Bigl\{ \mathbf{x} = -\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} + \lambda \mathbf{R}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} : \lambda \in \mathbb{R} \Bigr\} $$

projects to $\mathbf{u}$.

b. Plane Intersection: The plane constraint $\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{x} + c = 0$ uniquely determines the scalar $\lambda$, and hence the unique point $\mathbf{x}$.

Thus, the final answer is

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} = -\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} + \frac{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{t} - c}{\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{R}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}} \mathbf{R}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. } $$

B.3 Optional Board-Plane Residual#

The main formulation uses reprojection error. As an optional alternative, inverse ray-plane intersection gives the point where a back-projected image ray intersects a known plane, provided the ray is not parallel to the plane. This subsection returns to the local-to-world camera pose convention used in the main article.

For board $b$, its world-frame plane is

$$ n_b^\top X+c_b=0, $$

where

$$ n_b=R^B_b e_3, \qquad c_b=-n_b^\top t^B_b, \qquad e_3= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\0\\1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Camera $c$’s center in world coordinates is now directly:

$$ \boxed{ o_c=t^C_c. } $$

For homogeneous image coordinate

$$ \tilde u_{cp}= \begin{bmatrix} u_{cp}\\ v_{cp}\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, $$

the ray direction in camera coordinates is

$$ d_{cp}^{C} = K_c^{-1}\tilde u_{cp}. $$

The same direction in world coordinates is

$$ \boxed{ d_{cp}^{W} = R^C_c d_{cp}^{C}. } $$

The ray-plane intersection is

$$ \boxed{ \phi_{cp}(T^C_c,T^B_b) = o_c - \frac{n_b^\top o_c+c_b} {n_b^\top d_{cp}^{W}} d_{cp}^{W}. } $$

The board-generated world point is

$$ \boxed{ X_p^W=R^B_b q_p+t^B_b. } $$

The post-style residual would be

$$ \boxed{ r_{cp}^{\text{ray}} = P_b^\top \left[ \phi_{cp}(T^C_c,T^B_{\beta(p)}) - \left( R^B_{\beta(p)} q_p+t^B_{\beta(p)} \right) \right], } $$

where

$$ P_b= \begin{bmatrix} R^B_b e_1 & R^B_b e_2 \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb R^{3\times2} $$

is the tangent basis of the board plane.

I recommend using the tangent-plane residual, not the full 3D residual, because both points are supposed to lie on the board plane and the meaningful error is along the board surface.

However, the reprojection objective is usually preferable because pixel noise is naturally modeled in image coordinates. The ray-plane residual requires converting pixel uncertainty into board-plane uncertainty, which depends on viewing angle and can become ill-conditioned when

$$ n_b^\top d_{cp}^{W}\approx 0. $$

Appendix C. Alternative Design Formulations#

The main article uses camera-uncertainty minimization. This appendix retains alternative criteria and derivations for readers who need a different design objective or a discrete placement budget.

C.1 Single-Board Objective and Tolerance Scaling#

A direct next-board-placement problem is

$$ \boxed{ (T^B_b)^* = \underset{T^B_b\in\mathcal F}{\arg\min} \; \operatorname{tr} \left( D\Sigma_C(T^B_b)D^\top \right), } $$

where $\mathcal F$ is the feasible set of board poses, and $D$ normalizes translation and rotation by required tolerances. For a single camera pose, one example is

$$ D = \operatorname{diag} \left( 1/\tau_x,\, 1/\tau_y,\, 1/\tau_z,\, 1/\tau_{\mathrm{roll}},\, 1/\tau_{\mathrm{pitch}},\, 1/\tau_{\mathrm{yaw}} \right). $$

For multiple cameras, use the corresponding block-diagonal normalization.

Other useful criteria are the D-optimal score

$$ \boxed{ \max_{T^B_b\in\mathcal F} \log\det\Lambda_C(T^B_b), } $$

which reduces the overall uncertainty volume, and the E-optimal score

$$ \boxed{ \max_{T^B_b\in\mathcal F} \lambda_{\min} \left( D^{-T}\Lambda_C(T^B_b)D^{-1} \right), } $$

which improves the least observable camera-pose direction. For extrinsic calibration, the E-optimal criterion is often the more practical one, because a calibration can have a small average error while still containing one poorly constrained combination, such as camera height and pitch.

C.2 Overview of Alternative Criteria#

Other experimental-design objectives can be substituted:

$$ \text{D-optimal:}\qquad \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\max}\; \log\det \Lambda_\theta(s) - \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s), $$$$ \text{E-optimal:}\qquad \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\max}\; \lambda_{\min} \left( D^{-T}\Lambda_\theta(s)D^{-1} \right) - \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s), $$

or a worst-camera objective:

$$ \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min}\; \max_{c\in\mathcal C} \operatorname{tr} \left( D_c \Sigma_{\theta_c}(s)D_c^\top \right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s). $$

C.3 Detailed Alternative Upper-Level Criteria#

The A-optimal objective in (BL-A) minimizes the normalized trace of the predicted camera covariance. Other criteria are useful in different production settings.

D-optimal placement minimizes the uncertainty volume:

$$ \boxed{ s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\max} \; \log\det \Lambda_\theta(s) - \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s). } \tag{BL-D} $$

Equivalently,

$$ s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min} \; - \log\det \Lambda_\theta(s) + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s). $$

E-optimal placement improves the weakest observable camera-pose direction:

$$ \boxed{ s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\max} \; \lambda_{\min} \left( D^{-T} \Lambda_\theta(s) D^{-1} \right) - \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s). } \tag{BL-E} $$

This is often useful for camera extrinsic calibration because the failure mode is usually not the average error, but one poorly constrained direction, such as height-pitch coupling or lateral-yaw coupling.

If every camera must satisfy a production tolerance, use a worst-camera objective:

$$ \boxed{ s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min} \; \max_{c\in\mathcal C} \operatorname{tr} \left( D_c \Sigma_{\theta_c}(s) D_c^\top \right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}C_{\mathrm{place}}(s). } \tag{BL-worst} $$

This avoids a placement that helps some cameras but leaves one camera poorly calibrated.

C.4 Abstract Bilevel Form and Objective Choices#

Putting the lower and upper levels together gives the clean abstract formulation:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min} &\quad \mathcal U(x^\star(s),s) \\ \text{s.t.} &\quad x^\star(s) \in \underset{x}{\arg\min} \; \mathcal L(x;s), \end{aligned} } \tag{BL} $$

where

$$ x= \left( \{T_c^C\}_{c=1}^C, \{T_b^B\}_{b=1}^B \right), $$

and $\mathcal L(x;s)$ is the joint calibration objective. The upper objective can be chosen according to the available information.

If the goal is truly optimized residual, use

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal U_{\mathrm{res}}(x^\star,s) = \mathbb E \left[ \frac{1}{|\mathcal E(s)|} \mathcal L(x^\star(s);s) \right]. } $$

If the study is a simulation and the ground-truth camera poses are known, use the actual camera-pose error:

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal U_{\mathrm{pose}}(x^\star,s) = \mathbb E \left[ \sum_c \left\| \operatorname{Log} \left( (T_c^{C,\mathrm{true}})^{-1} (T_c^C)^\star(s) \right) \right\|_{D_c^\top D_c}^2 \right]. } $$

For a real calibration station, the most practical design objective is information-based:

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal U_{\mathrm{info}}(s) = \operatorname{tr} \left( D\Lambda_\theta(s)^{-1}D^\top \right). } $$

For EOL board-placement design, $\mathcal U_{\mathrm{info}}(s)$ is preferable to optimized residual because it directly measures predicted camera-pose uncertainty.

C.5 Why Optimized Residual Is Not the Placement Objective#

If the upper level literally minimizes the final optimized residual, the bilevel problem is

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min} &\quad \mathbb E_{\epsilon} \left[ \mathcal L(x^\star(s,\epsilon);s,\epsilon) \right] + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}\,C_{\mathrm{place}}(s) \\ \text{s.t.} &\quad x^\star(s,\epsilon) \in \underset{x}{\arg\min} \; \mathcal L(x;s,\epsilon). \end{aligned} } \tag{BL-residual} $$

The normalized version is usually better:

$$ \boxed{ s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min} \; \mathbb E_{\epsilon} \left[ \frac{1}{|\mathcal E(s)|} \mathcal L(x^\star(s,\epsilon);s,\epsilon) \right] + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}}\,C_{\mathrm{place}}(s). } \tag{BL-residual-normalized} $$

The normalization prevents a placement with fewer visible markers from appearing artificially better only because it has fewer residual terms. However, residual minimization is not the best placement-design objective. In the noise-free case, many feasible board placements can achieve nearly zero residual. A low optimized reprojection error does not necessarily mean that the camera pose is accurately estimated. A degenerate placement can fit its own observations while leaving some camera-pose directions weakly observable.

This is why the more useful second-level problem is an optimal experimental design problem: choose board placements that minimize predicted camera-pose uncertainty.

C.6 Residual Value Function and Envelope Derivative#

The residual-minimization bilevel problem can also be written with the value function

$$ V(s) = \min_x \mathcal L(x;s). $$

Then

$$ \boxed{ s^\star = \underset{s\in\mathcal S}{\arg\min} \; V(s). } \tag{VF} $$

If the lower-level solution is unique and smooth, the envelope theorem gives

$$ \boxed{ \nabla_s V(s) = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial s} \left( x^\star(s);s \right). } $$

This is computationally convenient because the derivative of $x^\star(s)$ is not needed when the upper objective is exactly the optimized lower-level loss. For calibration placement design, however, $V(s)$ is usually not the best objective because minimized residual can be small even when the pose estimate is uncertain.

C.7 Fixed-Budget Discrete Board Selection#

The 40-board replacement experiment in Section 4.5 is a discrete, fixed-budget approximation of the board-placement design problem. Let $\mathcal G$ be the finite set of candidate perimeter-board poses, let $\mathcal A\subset\mathcal G$ be the selected board set, and let $|\mathcal A|=40$. For each candidate $g$, compute its marginalized camera-pose information contribution

$$ \Delta\Lambda_g = J_{c,g}^\top W_g J_{c,g} - J_{c,g}^\top W_g J_{b,g} \left( J_{b,g}^\top W_g J_{b,g} \right)^{-1} J_{b,g}^\top W_g J_{c,g}. $$

Then the fixed-budget subset problem is

$$ \mathcal A^\star = \underset{\mathcal A\subset\mathcal G,\;|\mathcal A|=40}{\arg\min} \; \operatorname{tr} \left[ D \left( \Lambda_0 + \sum_{g\in\mathcal A} \Delta\Lambda_g \right)^{-1} D^\top \right] + \lambda_{\mathrm{cost}} \sum_{g\in\mathcal A} C_{\mathrm{place}}(g), \tag{SUB} $$

subject to the same spacing, visibility, and placement constraints. The greedy swap procedure in Section 4.5 starts from the uniform set $\mathcal A_0$, evaluates replacements

$$ r\in\mathcal A,\qquad g\in\mathcal G\setminus\mathcal A, $$

and accepts the swap if

$$ U(\mathcal A-\{r\}+\{g\}) < U(\mathcal A), $$

where $U(\mathcal A)$ is the A-optimal trace objective in (SUB). This is why the experiment uses the solution of the initial 40-board calibration: the solved state gives the Jacobian, the Schur-marginalized camera information, and the covariance metric used to decide which physical board placements should be replaced.

Appendix D. Two-Camera Bundle-Adjustment Special Case#

Suppose the board marker coordinates in the board frame are $\{ \mathbf{x}_i^g \}_{i=1}^N$. The measured marker points in the world frame are $\{ \mathbf{x}_i^o \}_{i=1}^N$. The observed image markers in camera $c$ are $\{\mathbf{u}_{ci}\}$.

A standard bundle-adjustment objective should minimize pixel reprojection error, not a 3D inverse-ray-plane error. Let the board pose be

$$ \mathbf{T}_0= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R}_0 & \mathbf{t}_0\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, $$

so the board-generated world point is

$$ \mathbf{x}_i= \mathbf{R}_0\mathbf{x}_i^g+\mathbf{t}_0. $$

Let camera $c$ have world-to-camera extrinsic $(\mathbf{R}_c,\mathbf{t}_c)$ and intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{K}_c$. A clean two-camera objective is

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{T}_0,\{\mathbf{R}_c,\mathbf{t}_c\}} &\quad \sum_{c\in\{1,2\}} \sum_{i\in C_c} \left\| \mathbf{u}_{ci} - \Pi\left( \mathbf{K}_c \left[ \mathbf{R}_c (\mathbf{R}_0\mathbf{x}_i^g+\mathbf{t}_0) + \mathbf{t}_c \right] \right) \right\|_{\Sigma_{u,ci}^{-1}}^2 \\ &\quad+ \sum_i \left\| \mathbf{R}_0\mathbf{x}_i^g+\mathbf{t}_0-\mathbf{x}_i^o \right\|_{\Sigma_{x,i}^{-1}}^2 . \end{aligned} } $$

The shared board pose already couples the two cameras: if both cameras observe marker $i$, both residuals use the same physical point $\mathbf{R}_0\mathbf{x}_i^g+\mathbf{t}_0$. Therefore a separate term such as

$$ \left\| \phi_1(\mathbf{u}_{1i})-\phi_2(\mathbf{u}_{2i}) \right\|^2 $$

where $\phi_c$ is ray-plane intersection, is not an independent measurement. It is algebraically dependent on the same image observations and shared board or plane geometry, and it can double-count the same observations. The general multi-board formulation in Section 1 includes this two-camera reprojection objective as a special case.

Appendix E. ADMM and Bundle Adjustment Comparison#

E.1 Conclusion#

For end-of-line multi-camera calibration, sparse factor-graph bundle adjustment is better than ADMM when all observations can be processed on one machine. It solves the physical estimation problem directly, exploits its sparse camera-board structure, converges more reliably, and avoids consensus variables, dual variables, and penalty-parameter tuning.

ADMM remains useful when distribution is a hard system requirement. It should be selected for data locality, parallel ownership, or communication constraints—not because it is expected to produce a more accurate calibration than centralized bundle adjustment.

The calibration formulation and simulation are given in Section 1 and Section 4.

E.2 Same Estimation Problem, Different Solver Organization#

Both approaches can minimize the same physical objective:

$$ \min_{\{T_b^B\},\{T_c^C\}} \sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E} \left\| \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} \left[ \Pi\left(K_cP_3(T_c^C)^{-1}T_{\beta(p)}^B\bar q_p\right)-u_{cp} \right] \right\|^2 + \sum_b\phi_b(T_b^B) + \sum_c\psi_c(T_c^C). $$

The variables are camera poses $T_c^C$ and board poses $T_b^B$. Each reprojection factor connects one camera pose to one board pose through an observed marker point.

Factor-graph bundle adjustment keeps these physical variables in one sparse nonlinear least-squares problem. ADMM introduces local copies and consensus constraints so that parts of the problem can be solved separately.

E.3 ADMM Formulation#

The most general split for sparse visibility is point-level consensus. Let

$$ X_p(T_{\beta(p)}^B)=R_{\beta(p)}^Bq_p+t_{\beta(p)}^B $$

be the world position of marker point $p$, generated by the pose of its board $\beta(p)$. For every observed camera-point edge $(c,p)\in\mathcal E$, introduce a camera-local world-frame copy

$$ Y_{cp}\in\mathbb R^3, \qquad \bar Y_{cp}=[Y_{cp}^{\top},1]^{\top}. $$

Use the observation neighborhoods

$$ \mathcal N(c)=\{p:(c,p)\in\mathcal E\}, \qquad \mathcal N(p)=\{c:(c,p)\in\mathcal E\}. $$

All local copies are tied to the physical board geometry by the consensus constraint

$$ \boxed{ Y_{cp}=X_p(T_{\beta(p)}^B) } \qquad (c,p)\in\mathcal E. $$

The camera-local reprojection loss is

$$ \boxed{ \ell_{cp}(T_c^C,Y_{cp})= \frac12 \left\| \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} \left[ \Pi\left(K_cP_3(T_c^C)^{-1}\bar Y_{cp}\right)-u_{cp} \right] \right\|^2. } $$

The point-copy ADMM problem is therefore

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \min_{\{T_c^C\},\{T_b^B\},\{Y_{cp}\}} &\quad \sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E}\ell_{cp}(T_c^C,Y_{cp}) + \sum_b\phi_b(T_b^B) + \sum_c\psi_c(T_c^C) \\ \text{s.t.} &\quad Y_{cp}=X_p(T_{\beta(p)}^B), \qquad (c,p)\in\mathcal E. \end{aligned} } \tag{A} $$

When consensus is satisfied, substituting $Y_{cp}=X_p(T_{\beta(p)}^B)$ into (A) recovers the centralized bundle-adjustment objective exactly. The split changes the optimization organization, not the desired physical solution.

Scaled Augmented Lagrangian#

For every observation edge, introduce a scaled dual variable $\lambda_{cp}\in\mathbb R^3$ and penalty $\mu_{cp}>0$. Omitting constants independent of the primal variables, the scaled augmented Lagrangian is

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mathcal L_{\mu}= &\sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E}\ell_{cp}(T_c^C,Y_{cp}) + \sum_b\phi_b(T_b^B) + \sum_c\psi_c(T_c^C) \\ &+ \sum_{(c,p)\in\mathcal E} \frac{\mu_{cp}}{2} \left\| Y_{cp}-X_p(T_{\beta(p)}^B)+\lambda_{cp} \right\|^2. \end{aligned} } \tag{AL} $$

This problem is nonconvex because it contains $SE(3)$ poses and perspective projection. ADMM’s standard convex convergence guarantees therefore do not apply globally; good initialization and well-scaled penalties remain important.

Camera-Local Update#

At iteration $k$, each camera independently updates its pose and point copies:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &(T_c^C)^{k+1}, \{Y_{cp}^{k+1}\}_{p\in\mathcal N(c)} \\ &\quad= \underset{T_c^C,\{Y_{cp}\}}{\arg\min} \Bigg[ \psi_c(T_c^C) + \sum_{p\in\mathcal N(c)} \ell_{cp}(T_c^C,Y_{cp}) \\ &\qquad\qquad+ \sum_{p\in\mathcal N(c)} \frac{\mu_{cp}}{2} \left\| Y_{cp}-X_p((T_{\beta(p)}^B)^k)+\lambda_{cp}^k \right\|^2 \Bigg]. \end{aligned} } \tag{ADMM-1} $$

This is a small nonlinear least-squares problem for one camera. The camera pose should use a Lie-group update, while each $Y_{cp}$ uses an ordinary Euclidean update. All camera subproblems can run in parallel.

Board Update#

Each board independently reconciles the camera-local point copies with its rigid marker geometry:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} (T_b^B)^{k+1}= \underset{T_b^B\in SE(3)}{\arg\min} \Bigg[ \phi_b(T_b^B) + \sum_{p\in\mathcal P_b} \sum_{c\in\mathcal N(p)} \frac{\mu_{cp}}{2} \left\| Y_{cp}^{k+1}-R_b^Bq_p-t_b^B+\lambda_{cp}^{k} \right\|^2 \Bigg]. \end{aligned} } \tag{ADMM-2} $$

With isotropic point priors, this update is a weighted rigid-alignment problem and can be solved by weighted Kabsch. With anisotropic covariance or a general pose prior, it is a small six-degree-of-freedom LM problem. All board updates can also run in parallel.

Dual Update and Stopping Criteria#

For every camera-point edge, update the scaled dual variable by

$$ \boxed{ \lambda_{cp}^{k+1}=\lambda_{cp}^{k}+ Y_{cp}^{k+1}-X_p((T_{\beta(p)}^B)^{k+1}). } \tag{ADMM-3} $$

The primal and dual residuals can be written as

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} r_{p,cp}^{k+1} &=Y_{cp}^{k+1}-X_p((T_{\beta(p)}^B)^{k+1}), \\ r_{d,cp}^{k+1} &=\mu_{cp} \left[ X_p((T_{\beta(p)}^B)^{k+1})-X_p((T_{\beta(p)}^B)^k) \right]. \end{aligned} } $$

Stop only when both aggregated norms $\|r_p\|$ and $\|r_d\|$ satisfy their absolute and relative tolerances. If residual balancing changes $\mu_{cp}$, the scaled dual variable must be rescaled consistently.

The complete point-copy iteration is:

  1. Solve all camera-local problems in parallel.
  2. Solve all board-consensus problems in parallel.
  3. Update all scaled dual variables.
  4. Evaluate the primal and dual residuals.
  5. Adjust penalties if necessary and repeat.

Pose-Copy ADMM Variant#

When every visible camera-board pair contains enough well-distributed markers, one local board pose can replace all point copies for that incidence. Define

$$ \mathcal A= \left\{ (c,b):\exists p\in\mathcal P_b\text{ with }(c,p)\in\mathcal E \right\}, $$

with

$$ \mathcal N_B(c)=\{b:(c,b)\in\mathcal A\}, \qquad \mathcal N_B(b)=\{c:(c,b)\in\mathcal A\}. $$

Let $T_{b|c}^B$ be camera $c$’s local copy of board $b$. The split problem becomes

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \min_{\{T_c^C\},\{T_{b|c}^B\},\{T_b^B\}} &\quad \sum_c \left[ \psi_c(T_c^C)+ \sum_{b\in\mathcal N_B(c)}f_{cb}(T_c^C,T_{b|c}^B) \right] + \sum_b\phi_b(T_b^B) \\ \text{s.t.} &\quad T_{b|c}^B=T_b^B, \qquad (c,b)\in\mathcal A, \end{aligned} } \tag{PC} $$

where

$$ f_{cb}(T_c^C,T_b^B)= \frac12 \sum_{p\in\mathcal P_b:(c,p)\in\mathcal E} \left\| \Sigma_{u,cp}^{-1/2} \left[ \Pi\left(K_cP_3(T_c^C)^{-1}T_b^B\bar q_p\right)-u_{cp} \right] \right\|^2. $$

Pose consensus must be measured on the manifold rather than by subtracting transformation matrices. A suitable tangent-space disagreement is

$$ \Delta(Z,X)=\operatorname{Log}(Z^{-1}X)^{\vee}\in\mathbb R^6. $$

The corresponding consensus penalty for incidence $(c,b)$ is

$$ \frac{\rho_{cb}}{2} \left\| \Delta(T_b^B,T_{b|c}^B)+u_{cb} \right\|_{M_B}^{2}, $$

where $M_B$ scales rotational and translational components consistently. Camera workers update $(T_c^C,T_{b|c}^B)$, board workers update $T_b^B$, and the scaled dual update is

$$ u_{cb}^{k+1}=u_{cb}^k+ \Delta((T_b^B)^{k+1},(T_{b|c}^B)^{k+1}). $$

Pose-copy ADMM communicates one six-degree-of-freedom pose per visible camera-board incidence. Point-copy ADMM communicates one three-dimensional point per camera-point observation. The pose-copy form can therefore communicate much less state, but its local pose is weakly constrained when a camera sees too few or poorly distributed points on a board.

E.5 Why Bundle Adjustment Is Better Here#

It preserves the natural factor graph#

The calibration graph is already sparse. A reprojection factor touches only one camera pose and one board pose. Sparse bundle adjustment uses this structure directly instead of expanding the state with a copy of every shared point or board pose.

It uses second-order coupling immediately#

The centralized normal equations contain the camera-board coupling required to update the complete system coherently. Schur elimination can marginalize either the board variables or the camera variables while retaining their information contribution.

ADMM exposes the same coupling gradually through repeated consensus updates. That decomposition is useful across machines, but it is unnecessary overhead when the full sparse system is locally available.

It is easier to tune and diagnose#

Bundle adjustment requires a sensible initialization, residual weighting, robust losses when needed, and an LM or trust-region policy. ADMM requires all of those choices plus consensus penalties, rotation-versus-translation scaling, primal and dual stopping tolerances, and sometimes adaptive penalty updates.

With bundle adjustment, covariance inspection, observability analysis, marginalization, and residual diagnostics also operate directly on the physical factor graph.

It was faster in the reference experiment#

In the 8-camera, 24-board simulation, the three methods reached nearly the same solution, but centralized bundle adjustment was substantially faster in the serial reference implementation:

What This Demonstrates#

The simulation illustrates four points from the formulation:

  1. The primitive observation is still the camera-point edge $(c,p)$, even though the optimized variables are camera and board poses.
  2. Centralized BA directly uses the sparse camera-board structure and gives the reference solution.
  3. Point-level ADMM reaches nearly the same solution over all tested scales, but in this serial implementation it has no accuracy or runtime advantage over sparse BA. Its value is exposing camera-parallel and board-parallel subproblems.
  4. Pose-copy ADMM is also valid when each camera-board incidence is well constrained. It gives similar accuracy while communicating far fewer consensus variables, but it still does not beat centralized BA in this serial Python implementation.

For production EOL calibration, the centralized sparse solve is the default choice when all data can be processed on one machine. ADMM splits are mainly useful when the calibration system is distributed, when cameras are processed by separate workers, when communication is constrained, or when the board update must remain a simple rigid-alignment module.

E.7 When ADMM Is Still Appropriate#

ADMM becomes reasonable when at least one of the following is a real requirement:

  1. Images or extracted observations must remain on separate camera processors.
  2. The computation is physically distributed and only compact consensus state may be exchanged.
  3. Independent camera workers must continue operating despite limited central compute.
  4. The calibration graph is too large for the available centralized memory.
  5. Organizational or privacy boundaries prevent pooling the observations.

Point-level ADMM is more tolerant of camera-board incidences with only a few observed markers, but it communicates many three-dimensional point copies. Pose-copy ADMM communicates much less state when every visible camera-board pair has enough well-distributed markers to constrain a local six-degree-of-freedom pose.

Even in a distributed design, factor graphs remain useful: each ADMM worker can solve its local nonlinear least-squares subproblem with a factor-graph optimizer. In that architecture, ADMM is the coordination layer, not a replacement for bundle adjustment inside each worker.

E.8 Recommendation#

Use sparse factor-graph bundle adjustment by default for production EOL calibration. Move to ADMM only after identifying a concrete distribution or communication constraint that the centralized solver cannot satisfy.

The selection rule is therefore simple:

$$ \text{centralized data}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{sparse factor-graph bundle adjustment}, $$$$ \text{mandatory distributed ownership}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{ADMM with factor-graph local solves}. $$
